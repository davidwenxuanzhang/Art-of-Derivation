## General Hessian Criterion

::::tip General Hessian Criterion

For a function $f: \mathbb{R}^n\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$, we take $p\in \mathbb{R}^n$ such that $\nabla {f(p)}=0$, and this is a necessary condition for being a local max/min.

At any point in $\mathbb{R}^n$, we can construct the Hessian matrix:

$$
\operatorname{Hess}(f)(p)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\partial_{x_1}^2 f(p) & \cdots & \partial_{x_n}\partial_{x_1}f(p)\\
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\
\partial_{x_1}\partial_{x_n}f(p) & \cdots & \partial_{x_n}^2 f(p)
\end{bmatrix}
$$
We can look at $\mathbf{d}=(d_1,d_2,\dots,d_n)$, where $d_i$, called the $i$-th **leading principal minor**, is the determinant of the top-left $i\times i$ submatrix.

$$
d_1=\partial_{x_1}^{2}f(p)\qquad d_2=\det
\begin{bmatrix}
\partial_{x_1}^2 f(p) & \partial_{x_1}\partial_{x_2}f(p)\\
\partial_{x_2}\partial_{x_1}f(p) & \partial_{x_2}^2 f(p)
\end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
d_i=\det\begin{bmatrix}
\partial_{x_1}^2 f(p) & \cdots & \partial_{x_i}\partial_{x_1}f(p)\\
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\
\partial_{x_1}\partial_{x_i}f(p) & \cdots & \partial_{x_i}^2 f(p)
\end{bmatrix}
$$

-  If $d_i>0$ for all $i$, then $p$ is a local minimum.

-  If the signs alternate starting with $d_1<0$, that is, $d_1<0,\ d_2>0,\ d_3<0,\dots$ (equivalently $(-1)^i d_i>0$ for all $i$), then $p$ is a local maximum.

-  If $d_n\ne 0$ and neither of the above holds, then $p$ is a saddle point.
-  If $d_n=0$, then the Hessian criterion is inconclusive.

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

Since $\nabla f(p)=0$, the first-order terms of the Taylor expansion vanish, and the conditions above control the sign of the degree $2$ polynomial:

$$
T_f^{(2)} (p)(\varepsilon_1,\dots,\varepsilon_n)-f(p)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\partial_{x_i}\partial_{x_j}f(p)\varepsilon_i \varepsilon_j
$$

</details>

:::warning Example
Given a function, we are looking for its local extrema:
$$
f(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2+xy+yz
$$

Find the critical points:
$$
\nabla f(x,y,z)=(2x+y,x+2y+z,2z+y)=0\iff (x,y,z)=(0,0,0)
$$
Find the Hessian Matrix:
$$
\operatorname{Hess}(f)(x,y,z)=
\begin{bmatrix}
2 & 1 & 0\\
1 & 2 & 1\\
0 & 1 & 2
\end{bmatrix}
$$
Use the General Hessian Criterion:
$$
d_1=2\qquad d_2=\det
\begin{bmatrix}
2 & 1\\
1 & 2
\end{bmatrix}
= 4 - 1 = 3
$$

$$
\begin{align*}
d_3 = \det\begin{bmatrix}
2 & 1 & 0\\
1 & 2 & 1\\
0 & 1 & 2
\end{bmatrix}
&= 2\det
\begin{bmatrix}
2 & 1\\
1 & 2
\end{bmatrix}
- 1\det
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 1\\
0 & 2
\end{bmatrix}
+ 0\\
&=2\cdot 3-1\cdot 2=4
\end{align*}
$$
$$
\mathbf{d}=(2,3,4)\implies d_i \text{'s are positive}
$$

$\implies (0,0,0)$ is a local minimum (in this case, it is also the global minimum).

:::

:::danger Remark

Why is the Hessian test inconclusive if $d_n=0$?

Morally, if $d_n=0$, then the sign of $f(x)-f(p)$ is not controlled by $\sum_{i,j}\partial_{x_i}\partial_{x_j}f(p)\varepsilon_i \varepsilon_j$.

:::warning Example

$$
\begin{align*}
f(x,y,z)&=x^2+y^2+xy+z^4\implies d_3=0 \text{ and } (0,0,0)\text{ is a local min}\\
g(x,y,z)&=x^2+y^2+xy-z^4\implies d_3=0 \text{ and } (0,0,0)\text{ is not a local min}
\end{align*}
$$

:::

## Considered Domain

So far, we have always supposed that $f:\mathbb{R}^n\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ was defined over some open set $D$.

More precisely, we are only looking for min/max over an **open subset** $D\subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$.

::::tip Open Set 

$D\subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is open if for every point $p\in D$ there is a small ball $B_p(\varepsilon)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^n: \|x-p \|<\varepsilon  \}\subseteq D$ for some $\varepsilon > 0$.

:::warning Non-Example

$$
B_1=\{ (x,y,z) \text{ such that } x^2+y^2+z^2\le 1  \}
$$

is not open, because if $p\in B_1$ with $\|p\|=1$, then there is no $B_p(\varepsilon)\subseteq B_1$.

:::

:::warning Example

$$
B_1=\{ (x,y,z) \text{ such that } x^2+y^2+z^2< 1  \}
$$

is open.

In practice, we are often interested in finding the min/max of a function over a domain that is not open, where we cannot just use derivatives to find local extrema.

:::

::::

### Case 0

:::warning Case 0

The domain is of the form $D=\{g=0 \}$ for some function $g: \mathbb{R}^n\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$; in other words, given $f:\mathbb{R}^n\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$, we want to find the min/max of $f$ along a level set of $g$. For example, if

$$
g(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2-1
$$

we need to look for the min/max of $f$ along the unit sphere.

:::

:::danger General Remark

By a local min/max on a domain $D\subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, we mean a point which is the minimum/maximum of the function in a small neighborhood in $D$.

:::

### Lagrange Multipliers


::::tip Lagrange Multipliers

Let $f:\mathbb{R}^n\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be a function and let the domain $D$ be a [level set](./9-29-2026.mdx#level-sets) of a function $g$, that is, $D=\{\mathbf{x}\in \mathbb{R}^n\text{ with }g(\mathbf{x})=k \}$.

Suppose that $\nabla g(\mathbf{x})\ne 0 \text{ for all } \mathbf{x}\in D$.

If $p\in D$ is a local min/max for $f$ over $D$, then $\nabla f(p)$ is parallel to $\nabla g(p)$, that is to say, $\nabla f(p)= \lambda \nabla g(p)$ for some $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$.

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

We will prove the contrapositive: if $\nabla f(p)\ne \lambda \nabla g(p)$ for every $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$, then $p$ is neither a local min nor a local max.

If $\nabla f(p)$ and $\nabla g(p)$ are not parallel, then we can consider $v$, the projection of $\nabla f(p)$ onto $T_p D$:

$$
v=\operatorname{proj}_{T_p D}\nabla f(p)\ne 0
$$

Now, consider a curve $\gamma: \mathbb{R}\rightarrow D$ such that $\gamma(0)=p$ and $\gamma^{\prime}(0)=v$.

If $p$ is a local minimum for $f$, then $0$ is a local minimum for $f\circ \gamma$, so its derivative at $0$ must vanish. However:

$$
\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}f\big( \gamma(t)\big)\right|_{t=0}= \nabla f\big( \gamma(0)\big)\cdot \gamma^{\prime}(0)=\nabla f(p)\cdot v =\|v\|^2\ne 0
$$

The same argument works for a local maximum. Therefore, the assumption that $\nabla f(p)$ and $\nabla g(p)$ are not parallel is wrong, and we get:

$$
\nabla f(p)= \lambda \nabla g(p)
$$
By the contrapositive of what we proved, if $p$ is a local min/max, then $\nabla f(p)$ is parallel to $\nabla g(p)$, as we wanted.
</details>
