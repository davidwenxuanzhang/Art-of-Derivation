9/3/2026 - Lecture 4

Disclaimer

The definitions and examples below are not meant to be rigorous or complete from a physics standpoint. This lecture is only for the purpose of applying curves to dynamical physics problems and getting familiar with these mathematical terms.

Dynamical Problems​

In physics, you often describe the motion of a particle subject to some force.

Notation

We will write r(t)\mathbf{r}(t)r(t) for the position vector of the particle, where ttt represents the time.

r(t)\mathbf{r}(t)r(t) can be seen as a parametrized curve.

The particle is subject to a force F(t)\mathbf{F}(t)F(t)

Newton's Law

For the force:

F(t)=ma(t)\mathbf{F}(t)=m\mathbf{a}(t)F(t)=ma(t)

where mmm is the mass of the particle, and a(t)\mathbf{a}(t)a(t) is the acceleration of the particle, also as:

a(t)=r′′(t)\mathbf{a}(t)=\mathbf{r}^{\prime \prime}(t)a(t)=r′′(t)

Circular Motion​

Circular Motion

Suppose the curve described by the particle of mass mmm is:

r1(t)=(cos⁡(t),sin⁡(t))r2(t)=(cos⁡(t),sin⁡(t),t)\begin{align*} \mathbf{r}_1(t)&=\big(\cos{(t)},\sin{(t)}\big)\\ \mathbf{r}_2(t)&=\big(\cos{(t)},\sin{(t)},t\big) \end{align*}r1​(t)r2​(t)​=(cos(t),sin(t))=(cos(t),sin(t),t)​
  1. Compute the force acting on the particle:

By Newton's Law F(t)=ma(t)\mathbf{F}(t)=m\mathbf{a}(t)F(t)=ma(t) in curve r1\mathbf{r}_1r1​ we get:

v1(t)=r1′(t)=(−sin⁡(t),cos⁡(t))a1(t)=r1′′(t)=(−cos⁡(t),−sin⁡(t))  ⟹  F(t)=ma1(t)=m(−cos⁡(t),−sin⁡(t))\begin{align*} \mathbf{v}_1(t)&=\mathbf{r}_1^{\prime }(t)=\big(-\sin{(t)},\cos{(t)}\big)\\ \mathbf{a}_1(t)&=\mathbf{r}_1^{\prime \prime}(t)=\big(-\cos{(t)},-\sin{(t)}\big)\\ \implies \mathbf{F}(t)&=m\mathbf{a}_1(t)=m\big(-\cos{(t)},-\sin{ (t)}\big) \end{align*}v1​(t)a1​(t)⟹F(t)​=r1′​(t)=(−sin(t),cos(t))=r1′′​(t)=(−cos(t),−sin(t))=ma1​(t)=m(−cos(t),−sin(t))​
  1. Compute v(t)⋅F(t)\mathbf{v}(t) \cdot \mathbf{F}(t)v(t)⋅F(t) and give an explanation for the value:
v(t)⋅F(t)=0  ⟹  v(t)⋅a(t)=0\begin{align*} \mathbf{v}(t) \cdot \mathbf{F}(t)&=0\\ \implies \mathbf{v}(t) \cdot \mathbf{a}(t)&=0\\ \end{align*}v(t)⋅F(t)⟹v(t)⋅a(t)​=0=0​  ⟹  ∥v(t)∥ is constant\implies \|\mathbf{v}(t)\| \text{ is constant}⟹∥v(t)∥ is constant

Below is the proof:

Derivation
(∥v(t)∥2)′=(v(t)⋅v(t))′=(v′(t)⋅v(t))+(v(t)⋅v′(t))(Dot Product Rule)=(a(t)⋅v(t))+(v(t)⋅a(t))=2(v(t)⋅a(t))=0\begin{align*} \left(\|\mathbf{v}(t)\|^2\right)^{\prime}&=\big(\mathbf{v}(t) \cdot \mathbf{v}(t)\big)^\prime\\ &=\big(\mathbf{v}^{\prime}(t) \cdot \mathbf{v}(t)\big)+\big(\mathbf{v}(t) \cdot \mathbf{v}^{\prime}(t)\big) &\qquad \text{(Dot Product Rule)}\\ &=\big(\mathbf{a}(t)\cdot \mathbf{v}(t) \big)+\big(\mathbf{v}(t)\cdot \mathbf{a}(t) \big)\\ &=2\big(\mathbf{v}(t)\cdot \mathbf{a}(t) \big)\\ &=0 \end{align*}(∥v(t)∥2)′​=(v(t)⋅v(t))′=(v′(t)⋅v(t))+(v(t)⋅v′(t))=(a(t)⋅v(t))+(v(t)⋅a(t))=2(v(t)⋅a(t))=0​(Dot Product Rule)

Therefore, ∥v(t)∥\|\mathbf{v}(t)\|∥v(t)∥ is constant.

■\blacksquare■

Angular Momentum​

Angular Momentum

For a particle moving along a curve, its angular momentum M(t)\mathbf{M}(t)M(t) is:

M(t)=m(r(t)×v(t))\mathbf{M}(t)=m\big( \mathbf{r}(t)\times \mathbf{v}(t)\big)M(t)=m(r(t)×v(t))
Note

The Angular Momentum measures how much a particle is rotating with respect to the origin.

In the case of r(t)=(cos⁡(t),sin⁡(t),0)\mathbf{r}(t)=\big(\cos{(t)},\sin{(t)},0 \big)r(t)=(cos(t),sin(t),0), both r(t)\mathbf{r}(t)r(t) and v(t)\mathbf{v}(t)v(t) lie on the xyxyxy-plane; therefore, M(t)\mathbf{M}(t)M(t) lies along the zzz-axis.

r(t)=(cos⁡(t),sin⁡(t),0)v(t)=(−sin⁡(t),cos⁡(t),0)\mathbf{r}(t)=\big(\cos{(t)},\sin{(t)},0 \big) \qquad \mathbf{v}(t)=\big(-\sin{(t)},\cos{(t)},0 \big)r(t)=(cos(t),sin(t),0)v(t)=(−sin(t),cos(t),0)M(t)=mr(t)×v(t)=m(0,0,1)\mathbf{M}(t)=m\mathbf{r}(t)\times\mathbf{v}(t)=m(0,0,1)M(t)=mr(t)×v(t)=m(0,0,1)

Torque​

Torque

When we take the derivative of angular momentum, we have:

ddtM(t)=M′(t)=r(t)×F(t)\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{M}(t)=\mathbf{M}^{\prime}(t)=\mathbf{r}(t)\times \mathbf{F}(t)dtd​M(t)=M′(t)=r(t)×F(t)

where r(t)×F(t)\mathbf{r}(t)\times \mathbf{F}(t)r(t)×F(t) is called the Torque

Derivation
M(t)=mr(t)×v(t)ddtM(t)=m(r′(t)×v(t)+r(t)×v′(t))(Cross Product Rule)=m(v(t)×v(t)+r(t)×a(t))=m(r(t)×a(t))(v(t)×v(t)=0)=r(t)×F(t)(Newton’s Law: F=ma)\begin{align*} \mathbf{M}(t)&=m\mathbf{r}(t)\times \mathbf{v}(t)\\ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{M}(t)&=m\big(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}(t)\times \mathbf{v}(t) +\mathbf{r}(t)\times \mathbf{v}^{\prime}(t)\big) &\qquad \text{(Cross Product Rule)}\\ &=m\big(\mathbf{v}(t)\times \mathbf{v}(t) +\mathbf{r}(t)\times \mathbf{a}(t)\big)\\ &=m\big(\mathbf{r}(t)\times \mathbf{a}(t)\big) & \qquad \big(\mathbf{v}(t)\times \mathbf{v}(t)=0\big)\\ &=\mathbf{r}(t)\times\mathbf{F}(t) &\qquad \big(\text{Newton's Law: } \mathbf{F}=m\mathbf{a}\big) \tag*{$\blacksquare$} \end{align*}M(t)dtd​M(t)​=mr(t)×v(t)=m(r′(t)×v(t)+r(t)×v′(t))=m(v(t)×v(t)+r(t)×a(t))=m(r(t)×a(t))=r(t)×F(t)​(Cross Product Rule)(v(t)×v(t)=0)(Newton’s Law: F=ma)​■​
Remarkable Particular Case

From the definition above, if the torque r(t)×F(t)\mathbf{r}(t)\times \mathbf{F}(t)r(t)×F(t) is 0\mathbf{0}0, then:

M(t) is constant\mathbf{M}(t) \text{ is constant}M(t) is constant

This will lead to another remarkable consequence that we will introduce below.

Kepler's Law​

Kepler's Law

If M(t)\mathbf{M}(t)M(t) is constant, then the trajectory of r(t)\mathbf{r}(t)r(t) is planar; that is to say, r(t)\mathbf{r}(t)r(t) lies on a plane.

Derivation

Suppose this is true; then M(t)=M\mathbf{M}(t)=\mathbf{M}M(t)=M (a constant vector) should be the normal direction of the plane. Therefore, our conjecture becomes

r(t)⋅M(t)=constant\mathbf{r}(t)\cdot \mathbf{M}(t)=\text{constant}r(t)⋅M(t)=constant

Let's compute (r(t)⋅M)′\big(\mathbf{r}(t)\cdot \mathbf{M}\big)^{\prime}(r(t)⋅M)′:

(r(t)⋅M(t))′=v(t)⋅M(t)+r(t)⋅M′(t)(Dot Product Rule)=v(t)⋅M(t)(M′(t)=0)=v(t)⋅(m(r(t)×v(t)⏟orthogonal to v(t)))=0\begin{align*} \big(\mathbf{r}(t)\cdot \mathbf{M}(t)\big)^{\prime}&=\mathbf{v}(t)\cdot \mathbf{M}(t)+\mathbf{r}(t)\cdot \mathbf{M}^{\prime}(t) &\qquad \text{(Dot Product Rule)}\\ &=\mathbf{v}(t)\cdot \mathbf{M}(t) &\qquad \big(\mathbf{M}^{\prime}(t)=\mathbf{0}\big)\\ &=\mathbf{v}(t)\cdot\Bigl(m\big(\underbrace{\mathbf{r}(t)\times \mathbf{v}(t)}_{\text{orthogonal to } \mathbf{v}(t)}\big)\Bigr)\\ &=0 \end{align*}(r(t)⋅M(t))′​=v(t)⋅M(t)+r(t)⋅M′(t)=v(t)⋅M(t)=v(t)⋅(m(orthogonal to v(t)r(t)×v(t)​​))=0​(Dot Product Rule)(M′(t)=0)  ⟹  the curve lies on a plane with normal direction M■\implies \text{the curve lies on a plane with normal direction } \mathbf{M} \tag*{$\blacksquare$}⟹the curve lies on a plane with normal direction M■

Purely Math Statement​

Purely Math Statement

Consider a curve γ(t)\gamma(t)γ(t) such that γ(t)×γ′(t)\gamma(t)\times \gamma^{\prime}(t)γ(t)×γ′(t) is constant. Then the trajectory is planar; that is to say, γ(t)\gamma(t)γ(t) lies on a plane.

Derivation

Let n=γ(t)×γ′(t)\mathbf{n}=\gamma(t)\times \gamma^{\prime}(t)n=γ(t)×γ′(t) be this constant vector. As with Kepler's Law, we show γ(t)⋅n\gamma(t)\cdot \mathbf{n}γ(t)⋅n is constant:

(γ(t)⋅n)′=γ′(t)⋅n+γ(t)⋅n′(Dot Product Rule)=γ′(t)⋅n(n′=0)=γ′(t)⋅(γ(t)×γ′(t)⏟orthogonal to γ′(t))=0\begin{align*} \big(\gamma(t)\cdot \mathbf{n}\big)^{\prime}&=\gamma^{\prime}(t)\cdot \mathbf{n}+\gamma(t)\cdot \mathbf{n}^{\prime} &\qquad \text{(Dot Product Rule)}\\ &=\gamma^{\prime}(t)\cdot \mathbf{n} &\qquad \big(\mathbf{n}^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}\big)\\ &=\gamma^{\prime}(t)\cdot\Bigl(\underbrace{\gamma(t)\times \gamma^{\prime}(t)}_{\text{orthogonal to } \gamma^{\prime}(t)}\Bigr)\\ &=0 \end{align*}(γ(t)⋅n)′​=γ′(t)⋅n+γ(t)⋅n′=γ′(t)⋅n=γ′(t)⋅(orthogonal to γ′(t)γ(t)×γ′(t)​​)=0​(Dot Product Rule)(n′=0)  ⟹  the curve lies on a plane with normal direction n■\implies \text{the curve lies on a plane with normal direction } \mathbf{n} \tag*{$\blacksquare$}⟹the curve lies on a plane with normal direction n■
