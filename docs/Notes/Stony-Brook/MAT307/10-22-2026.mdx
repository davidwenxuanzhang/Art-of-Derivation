## Lagrange Multipliers

Last time, we introduced [Lagrange multipliers](./10-20-2026.mdx#lagrange-multipliers), which say:

If we want to find the local minimum or local maximum of an objective function $f$ subject to a constraint $g=k$ (with $\nabla g\ne 0$ on the constraint set), then:

$$
p \text{ is a local min or local max}\implies \nabla f(p)=\lambda \nabla g(p)
$$

This time, we will focus on their applications.

### Application of Lagrange Multipliers

:::warning Example 1

Consider $D$ the ellipse $D=\{ (x,y)\in \mathbb{R}^2\text{ such that } x^2+4y^2=1 \}$. Find the maximum area of a rectangle inscribed in the ellipse.

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

$D$ is the level set $\ell_1$ of $g(x,y)=x^2+4y^2$. For any point $p=(x,y)\in D$, there is a unique rectangle $R_{p}$, with sides parallel to the axes and a corner at $p$, with area:

$$
f(x,y)=4xy
$$

Then, we transform the problem into finding the maximum of $f$ over $D$. By Lagrange multipliers:

$$
p=(x,y)\text{ is a local min or local max}\implies \nabla f(p) =\lambda \nabla g(p) \text{ for some }\lambda
$$
Given $f(x,y)=4xy$, $g(x,y)=x^2+4y^2$, and $D=\{ (x,y)\in \mathbb{R}^2\text{ such that } x^2+4y^2=1 \}$:

$$
\nabla f(x,y)=(4y,4x) \qquad \nabla g(x,y)= (2x,8y)
$$

We can solve:

$$
\begin{cases}
    4y=\lambda 2x\\
    4x=\lambda 8y\\
    x^2+4y^2=1
\end{cases}
\implies 
\begin{cases}
    0=y(\lambda^2-1)\\
    x^2+4y^2=1
\end{cases}
$$

- When $y=0$

    Then $x=\pm 1$, and the rectangle is degenerate with area $0$, so it is not the maximum.

- When $\lambda=\pm 1$

    Then we get $x=\pm 2y$:

    $$
    \begin{cases}
        x=\pm 2y\\
        x^2+4y^2=1
    \end{cases}
    \implies 
    \begin{cases}
    x=\pm 2y\\
    y=\pm \sqrt{\frac{1}{8}}
    \end{cases}
    \implies
    \begin{cases}
    x=\pm \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \\
    y=\pm \frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}
    \end{cases}
    $$

So there are $4$ points in total that are potential local extrema:

$$
\left(\pm\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2},\ \pm\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\right)
$$

The maximum is attained when $x$ and $y$ have the same sign:

$$
f\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2},\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\right)=4\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}=4\cdot\frac{2}{8}=1
$$

Therefore, the maximum area of a rectangle inscribed in the ellipse is $1$.
</details>

:::

:::warning Example 2

Find the maximum volume of a parallelepiped inscribed in a sphere of radius $1$:

$$
D=\{x^2+y^2+z^2=1 \}
$$

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

A parallelepiped inscribed in a sphere must be a rectangular box. No matter which box we take, we can rotate it so that its edges are parallel to the $x$-, $y$-, and $z$-axes. If $(x,y,z)$ with $x,y,z>0$ is the corner in the first octant, the volume is:

$$
f(x,y,z)=8xyz
$$

Since $D$ is the level set $\ell_1$ of $g(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2$:

$$
\nabla f(x,y,z)=(8yz,8xz,8xy) \qquad \nabla g(x,y,z)=(2x,2y,2z)
$$
We can solve:

$$
\begin{cases}
    8yz=\lambda 2x\\
    8xz=\lambda 2y\\
    8xy=\lambda 2z\\
    x^2+y^2+z^2=1
\end{cases}
\implies
\begin{cases}
    8xyz=2\lambda x^2=2\lambda y^2=2\lambda z^2\\
    x^2+y^2+z^2=1
\end{cases}
$$

- When $x=0$, $y=0$, or $z=0$

    The box is degenerate with volume $0$, so it is not the maximum.

- When $x,y,z\ne 0$

    Then $\lambda\ne 0$, so $x^2=y^2=z^2$:

    $$
    \begin{cases}
        x^2=y^2=z^2\\
        x^2+y^2+z^2=1
    \end{cases}
    \implies
    3x^2=1
    \implies
    \begin{cases}
    x=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\\
    y=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\\
    z=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}
    \end{cases}
    $$

So the only Lagrange multiplier candidate with $x,y,z>0$ is $\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)$, and the box is a cube:

$$
f\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)=8\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)^3=\frac{8}{3\sqrt{3}}=\frac{8\sqrt{3}}{9}
$$

Therefore, the maximum volume of a parallelepiped inscribed in the unit sphere is $\frac{8\sqrt{3}}{9}$.

</details>

:::
