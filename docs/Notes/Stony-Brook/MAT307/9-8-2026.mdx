Then what is the length of the points of the curve between $\mathbf{r}(a)$ and $\mathbf{r}(b)$?

Here, we will approximate the length is via small segments; that is to say, we devide the entire curve from the interval $[a,b]$ into:

$$
t_0=a,\ t_1=a+\Delta{t}, \ \dots,\  t_i=a+i\Delta{t},\  \dots, \ t_n=b,\text{ where } \Delta{t}=\frac{b-a}{n}   
$$
Then, the length of the curve could approximate as:

$$
\ell_n=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\underbrace{\left\|{\mathbf{r}(t_i)-\mathbf{r}(t_{i-1})}\right\|}_{\mathclap{\text{the length of }i\text{th vector}}}
$$

From linear approximation:

$$
\begin{align*}
    \mathbf{r}(t_i)&\approx \mathbf{r}(t_{i-1})+\mathbf{r}^\prime(t_{i-1})\Delta{t}\\
    \implies \mathbf{r}(t_i)-\mathbf{r}(t_{i-1})&\approx \mathbf{r}^\prime(t_{i-1})\Delta{t}\\
    \implies \|{\mathbf{r}(t_i)-\mathbf{r}(t_{i-1})}\|&\approx \left\|\mathbf{r}^\prime(t_{i-1})\right\|\Delta{t}
\end{align*}
$$
Therefore:

$$
\ell_n\approx \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|\mathbf{r}^\prime(t_{i-1})\right\|\Delta{t}
$$

Then:

$$
\begin{align*}
    L&=\lim_{n\rightarrow \infty}\ell_n\\
     &=\lim_{n\rightarrow \infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|{\mathbf{r}(t_i)-\mathbf{r}(t_{i-1})}\right\|\\
     &=\lim_{n\rightarrow \infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\underbrace{\left\|\mathbf{r}^\prime(t_{i-1})\right\|\Delta{t}}_{\text{left Reimann Sum of }\|\mathbf{r}^{\prime}(t)\|}\\
     &=\int_{a}^{b}{\|\mathbf{r}^\prime (t)\|}\ \mathrm{d}t
\end{align*}
$$

## Arc Length Reparametrization

::::tip Arc Length Reparametrization

:::note Idea
Is there a special parametrization which is going through the image of the curve at constand speed that equal to 1? 
:::

$\mathbf{r}(t)$  is parametrized by arc length if:

$$
\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\mathbf{r}(s) \right\|=1
$$

::::

:::info Observation

There is a way to define the arclength parametrization for the curve $\mathbf{r}(t)$ which is not parametrizeal

We assume that $\mathbf{r}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^3$ is an injection, which different parameter $t$ will not correspond to the same point on the curve, and for that $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{r}(t)\ne 0$. Then, the arc length from $\mathbf{r}(t_0)$ to $\mathbf{r}(t_1)$ is:

$$
s(t)=\int_{t_0}^{t_1} \left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}\mathbf{r}(u) \right\|\mathrm{d}u
$$

that is to say, $s(t)$ is the length with a sign between $\mathbf{r}(t_0)$ and $\mathbf{r}(t_1)$

<hr />

$s(t)$ is bjected onto its image

:::

:::warning Exercise 1

Compute $\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{r}(s)\right\|$, and we are expecting 1

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

$$
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{r}(s)&=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{r}(t(s))\\
                                               &=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}{\mathbf{r\big(t(s)\big)}}\underbrace{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}t(s)}_{\text{we want this}}
\end{align*}
$$
As $s(t)$ is the arclength, then we can plug in the observation we made previously:

$$
\begin{align*}  
    \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}s(t)&=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{t_0}^{t_1} \left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}\mathbf{r}(u) \right\|\mathrm{d}u\\
                                      &=\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{r}(s) \right\| &\qquad \text{(Fundamental Theorem of Calculus)}\\
\implies \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}t(s)&=\frac{1}{\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{r}(s) \right\|}
\end{align*}
$$

Plug in back:

$$
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{r}(s)&=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}{\mathbf{r\big(t(s)\big)}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}t(s)\\
                                               &=\frac{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{r}\big(t(s)\big)}{{\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{r}\big(t(s)\big) \right\|}}
\end{align*}
$$
This is a unit vector, which has the magnitude of $1$ 
<div style={{ textAlign: 'right' }}>
  $\blacksquare$
</div>

</details>

### Unit Tangent Vector {#unit-tangent-vector}

::::tip Unit Tangent Vector

The **Unit Tangent Vector** to a arc length parametrization fo a curve $\mathbf{r}$ is:

$$
\mathbf{T}(s)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{\coloneqq} \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s} \mathbf{r}(s)
$$
:::info Property

$$
\mathbf{T}(s)\cdot \mathbf{T}^{\prime}(s)=0
$$

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

Because $\mathbf{T}(s)$ is the unit tangent vector:

$$
\begin{align*}
    \|\mathbf{T}(s)\| ^2=\mathbf{T}(s)\cdot \mathbf{T}(s)&=1\\
    \big( \mathbf{T}(s)\cdot  \mathbf{T}(s)\big)^{\prime}&=0\\
   2\big( \mathbf{T}(s)\cdot  \mathbf{T}^{\prime}(s)\big)&=0\\
   \big( \mathbf{T}(s)\cdot  \mathbf{T}^{\prime}(s)\big)&=0 \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
\end{align*}
$$

</details>

:::

### Normal Vector {#unit-normal-vector}

::::tip Normal Vector

The **Normal Vector** to an arc length parametrized curve is:

$$
\mathbf{N}(s)=\frac{\mathbf{T}^{\prime}(s)}{\|\mathbf{T}^{\prime}(s)\|}
$$
:::note
The normal vector tells us in whic direction the curve is "curving".
:::

### Curvature 

::::tip Curvature

The **Curvature** of a curve at a pooint $s$ is:

$$
\kappa(s)=\|\mathbf{T}^\prime(s)\|
$$
:::note
The curvature tells us how much the curve is "curving".
:::
