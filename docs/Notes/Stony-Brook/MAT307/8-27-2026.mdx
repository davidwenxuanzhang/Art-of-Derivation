**Geometrically**

Given two vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$, shown as below:

<svg viewBox="0 0 160 104" width="100%" style={{ maxWidth: '210px', height: 'auto', display: 'block', margin: '0.7rem auto 0.3rem' }} fill="none" stroke="currentColor" strokeWidth="2" strokeLinecap="round" strokeLinejoin="round" role="img" aria-label="Two vectors a and b from a common origin with the angle theta between them"><circle cx="20" cy="90" r="2" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"/><line x1="20" y1="90" x2="140" y2="60"/><polyline points="132,66 140,60 130,58"/><line x1="20" y1="90" x2="90" y2="25"/><polyline points="86,34 90,25 81,28"/><path d="M49.1,82.7 A30,30 0 0 0 42,69.6" strokeWidth="1.4" strokeOpacity="0.7"/><text x="142" y="70" fontSize="13" fontStyle="italic" fontWeight="700" fill="currentColor" stroke="none">a</text><text x="86" y="19" fontSize="13" fontStyle="italic" fontWeight="700" fill="currentColor" stroke="none">b</text><text x="55" y="73" fontSize="12" fontStyle="italic" fill="currentColor" stroke="none">θ</text></svg>

their **Dot Product** is:

$$
\mathbf{a}\cdot \mathbf{b}=\|\mathbf{a}\|\|\mathbf{b}\|\cos{\theta}
$$

  </div>

  <div
    style={{
      paddingLeft: "2rem",

**Analytically**

Given two vectors:
$$
\mathbf{v}=(v_1,v_2,v_3) \qquad \text{and}\qquad \mathbf{w}=(w_1,w_2,w_3)
$$
Their **Dot Product** is:

$$
\mathbf{v}\cdot \mathbf{w}=v_1w_1+v_2w_2+v_3w_3
$$
  </div>
</div>
Now we want to prove that the analytic and geometric definition agrees, below are some useful properties:
:::info Properties

- $\mathbf{v}\cdot \mathbf{w}= \mathbf{w}\cdot  \mathbf{v}$
- $\mathbf{v}\cdot (\mathbf{w}_1+\mathbf{w}_2)= \mathbf{v}\cdot \mathbf{w}_1+\mathbf{v}\cdot \mathbf{w}_2$
- $\mathbf{v}\cdot c\mathbf{w}= c(\mathbf{v}\cdot  \mathbf{w})$

:::

**From Vector**

$$
\begin{align*}
    \mathbf{a}+\mathbf{c}&=\mathbf{b}\implies \mathbf{c}=\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}\\ 
     \| \mathbf{c}\|^2&=\mathbf{c}\cdot \mathbf{c} \qquad \text{(Analytic Definition)}\\
                      &=(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a})\cdot (\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a})\\
                      &=\mathbf{b}\cdot \mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}\cdot \mathbf{b}-\mathbf{b}\cdot \mathbf{a}+\mathbf{a}\cdot \mathbf{a}\\
                      &=\| \mathbf{a}\|^2+\| \mathbf{b}\|^2-2(\mathbf{a}\cdot \mathbf{b})
\end{align*}
$$

  </div>

  <div
    style={{
      paddingLeft: "2rem",
    }}
  >

**From Geometry**

According to the [Law of Cosines](docs\Mathematics\Trigonometry\Law-of-Cosines.mdx): 

$$
\| \mathbf{c}\|^2=\| \mathbf{a}\|^2+\| \mathbf{b}\|^2-2\| \mathbf{a}\|\,\| \mathbf{b}\|\cos{\theta}
$$

  </div>
</div>

Both columns compute $\| \mathbf{c}\|^2$, so equating them and cancelling $\| \mathbf{a}\|^2+\| \mathbf{b}\|^2$ leaves

$$
\mathbf{a}\cdot \mathbf{b}=a_1b_1+a_2b_2=\| \mathbf{a}\|\,\| \mathbf{b}\|\cos{\theta} \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$

</details>

### Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

:::tip Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

Given two vectors $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{w}$, Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality says:

$$
\mathbf{v}\cdot \mathbf{w}\le \|\mathbf{v}\| \|\mathbf{w}\|
$$

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

Use the geometric definition:

$$
\begin{align*}
    \mathbf{v}\cdot \mathbf{w}&=\|\mathbf{v}\|\|\mathbf{w}\|\cos{\theta}\\
                              &\le \|\mathbf{v}\|\|\mathbf{w}\| \qquad{(\cos{\theta}< 1)} \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
\end{align*}
$$

</details>

### Lines

::::tip Lines in $\mathbb{R}^3$ (space)

<div
  style={{
    display: "grid",
    gridTemplateColumns: "1fr 1fr",
    gap: "0",
  }}
>
  <div
    style={{
      paddingRight: "2rem",
      borderRight: "1px solid var(--ifm-color-emphasis-300)",
    }}
  >

**Analytically**

We have the parametric equation of the line:

$$
\ell=\{P_0+t\mathbf{v},\ t\in \mathbb{R}\}
$$

If we start at a point $P_0=(x_1,y_0,z_0)$, and in the direction of $\mathbf{v}=(v_1,v_2,v_3)$, then the point on the line:

$$
(x,y,z)=(x_1,y_0,z_0)+t(v_1,v_2,v_3)
$$

$$ 
\left\{\begin{aligned}
x&=x_o+tv_1\\
y&=y_0+tv_2\\
z&=z_0+tv_3
\end{aligned}
\right.
$$
$$
t=\frac{x-x_0}{v_1}=\frac{y-y_0}{v_2}=\frac{z-z_0}{v_3}
$$
$$
\implies \ell = \{(x,y,z)\in \mathbb{R}^3 \text{s.t.} \frac{x-x_0}{v_1}=\frac{y-y_0}{v_2}=\frac{z-z_0}{v_3}\}
$$
  </div>

  <div
    style={{
      paddingLeft: "2rem",
    }}
  >

**Geometrically**

If a $P\in \ell$ and it can be written as $P=P_o+t\mathbf{v}$ for some $t\in \mathbb{R}$, then:

$$
\ell=\{P_0+t\mathbf{v},\ t\in \mathbb{R}\}
$$

:::info Example

If $P_0=(1,2,0)$, find the line through $P_0$ in the direction $\mathbf{v}=(2,0,1)$

Then, let $\mathbf{r}=(x,y,z)$:  

$$
\begin{align*}
    \ell: \mathbf{r}&=P_0+t\mathbf{v}\\
                    &=(1,2,0)+t(2,0,1)\\
                    &=(1+2t,2,t) \qquad  t  \in \mathbb{R}
\end{align*}
$$

:::

  </div>
</div>

  <div>

A plane $\Lambda$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is fixed by a **base point** $P_0\in \Lambda$ and a **normal vector** $\mathbf{n}\ne \mathbf{0}$ perpendicular to $\Lambda$.

A point $P$ lies on $\Lambda$ exactly when $\overrightarrow{P_0P}=P-P_0$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf{n}$:

$$
P\in \Lambda \iff \mathbf{n}\cdot (P-P_0)=0
$$

Writing $\mathbf{n}=(a,b,c)$ and $P=(x,y,z)$, this is

$$
a(x-x_0)+b(y-y_0)+c(z-z_0)=0,
$$

or in general form $ax+by+cz=d$ with $d=\mathbf{n}\cdot P_0$.

  </div>

</div>

## Recitation

:::tip Computation of Vectors

For $\mathbf{v}=(v_1,v_2,v_3)$ and $\mathbf{w}=(w_1,w_2,w_3)$ with angle $\theta$ between them:

<div style={{ width: 'fit-content', margin: '0 auto', overflowX: 'auto' }}>

| Operation | Formula |
|:---|:---|
| Scaling | $\lambda \mathbf{v}=(\lambda v_1,\ \lambda v_2,\ \lambda v_3)$ |
| Length | $\|\mathbf{v}\|=\sqrt{v_1^2+v_2^2+v_3^2}$ |
| Dot product | $\mathbf{v}\cdot \mathbf{w}=v_1w_1+v_2w_2+v_3w_3=\|\mathbf{v}\|\,\|\mathbf{w}\|\cos{\theta}$ |
| Cross product | $\mathbf{v}\times \mathbf{w}=(v_2w_3-v_3w_2,\ v_3w_1-v_1w_3,\ v_1w_2-v_2w_1)$ |
| Projection | $\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{w}=\left(\dfrac{\mathbf{v}\cdot \mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|^2}\right)\mathbf{v}$ |
| Orthogonal | $\mathbf{v}\perp \mathbf{w}\iff \mathbf{v}\cdot \mathbf{w}=0$ |

</div>

The cross product $\mathbf{v}\times \mathbf{w}$ is perpendicular to both $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{w}$ (direction by the right-hand rule), with length $\|\mathbf{v}\times \mathbf{w}\|=\|\mathbf{v}\|\,\|\mathbf{w}\|\sin{\theta}$ — the area of the parallelogram they span.

:::
