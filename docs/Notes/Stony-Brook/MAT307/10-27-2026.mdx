### Integral of a Vector Field Along a Curve

::::tip Integral of a Vector Field Along a Curve (Flow)

Given a curve $\gamma$:

$$
\gamma : [a,b]\longrightarrow \mathbb{R}^n
$$

Let $C$ be the image of the curve in $\mathbb{R}^n$ (forgetting the parametrization), then the integral of a vector field along a curve, also known as flow, is defined as:

$$
\int_{C}\mathbf{F}\cdot \mathrm{d}r \coloneqq \int_{a}^{b}\mathbf{F}\big(\gamma (t) \big)\cdot \gamma^{\prime}(t)\ \mathrm{d}t
$$

:::danger Remark

It does not depend on the choice of a parametrization (we saw it [last time](docs/Notes/Stony-Brook/MAT307/10-22-2026.mdx#flow)) provided that we choose a parametrization going through the curve once from $A=\gamma(a)$ and $B=\gamma(b)$

:::

::::warning Question

Given a vector field $\mathbf{u}$, when can we write $\mathbf{u}=\nabla g$? 

:::tip Potential

If such $g$ exists, it is called a **Potential** of $\mathbf{u}$. 

:::


<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

Let's try to find some conditions that $\mathbf{u}$ must satisfy in order to admit a potential.

- Suppose that $\mathbf{u}=\nabla g$ for some $g:\mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$

Consider a curve $C$ with a parametrization $\gamma$, what can I say about

$$
\begin{align*}
\int_{C}\mathbf{u}\cdot \mathrm{d}r &= \int_{a}^{b}\mathbf{u}\big(\gamma(t) \big)\cdot \gamma^{\prime}(t)\ \mathrm{d}t\\
&=\int_{a}^{b}\nabla g\big(\gamma(t) \big)\cdot \gamma^{\prime}(t)\ \mathrm{d}t

\end{align*}
$$

  </div>
</div>

<div style={{ marginLeft: '2rem' }}>

Notice that, by the [Chain Rule](docs/Notes/Stony-Brook/MAT307/10-6-2026.mdx#chain-rule), $\nabla g (t) \cdot \gamma^{\prime}(t)=g^{\prime}\big(\gamma(t) \big)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} g\big(\gamma(t) \big) $; by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

$$
\int_{a}^{b}\nabla g\big(\gamma(t) \big)\cdot \gamma^{\prime}(t)\ \mathrm{d}t=g\big(\gamma(t)\big)\Big|_{a}^{b}=g\big(\gamma(b)\big)-g\big(\gamma(a)\big)=g(B)-g(A)
$$

</div>

</details>

:::info Corollary 1

$\mathbf{u}=\nabla g\implies \int_{C}\mathbf{u}\cdot \mathrm{d}r=g(B)-g(A)$ depends only on the start and end point of the curve!

:::

:::info Corollary 2

$\mathrm{u}=\nabla g\implies \int_{C}\mathbf{u}\cdot \mathrm{d}r=0$ for any closed curve $C$, that is to say, $A=B$.

More importantly, by taking the contrapositive of this corollary: if I can find a closed curve $C$ such that $\int_{C}\mathbf{F}\cdot \mathrm{d}r\ne 0 $, then $F$ is not a gradient (does not admit a potential).

:::

### Conservative Vector Field

:::info Theorem

Given a vector field $\mathbf{u}$, the following are equivalent:
1. $\int_{C}\mathbf{u}\cdot \mathrm{d}r=0$ for any closed curve $C$
2. $\mathbf{u}$ admits a potential $g$.

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

We already saw that $2\implies 1$, so let's see how to do $1\implies 2$. 

We need to guess what $g$ is, so let's say $g(\mathbf{0})=0$, and we define

$$
g(p)\coloneqq \int_{C_p}\mathbf{u}\cdot \mathrm{d}r
$$

where $C_p$ is any curve joining $\mathbf{0}$ and $p$, and we need to check that this does not depend on the curve $C_p$

$$
\int_{C_p^{\prime}}\mathbf{u}\cdot \mathrm{d}r=\int_{\underbrace{C_p^{\prime}-C_p}_{=0}}\mathbf{u}\cdot \mathrm{d}r+\int_{C_p}\mathbf{u}\cdot \mathrm{d}r
$$

This tells us the value of $g(p)$ does not depend on the choice of the curve $C_p$

Now we have a good candidate for the potential, but we still need to check

$$
\nabla g=\mathbf{u},\qquad \mathbf{u}=(u_1,\dots,u_n)
$$
I will do the $2$-dimensional case, $\mathbf{u}=(u_1,u_2)$. For the scalar components $u_1$ and $u_2$, we need to check

$$
\partial_{x}g=u_1,\qquad \partial_{y}(g)=u_2
$$

$$
\begin{align*}
    \partial_{x}g(p)=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{g(p+\varepsilon \mathbf{e}_1)-g(p)}{\varepsilon}\\
    \partial_{y}g(p)=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{g(p+\varepsilon \mathbf{e}_2)-g(p)}{\varepsilon}
\end{align*}
$$

where $\mathbf{e}_i$ is a vector of the [standard basis](docs/Notes/Stony-Brook/MAT307/9-15-2026.mdx#standard-basis)

  </div>
</div>

$C_p$ is any curve joining from $\mathbf{0}$ to $p$, and $C_{\varepsilon_1}$ is the straight segment joining $p$ to $p+\varepsilon\mathbf{e}_1$

$$
g(p+\varepsilon\mathbf{e}_1)=\int_{C_p\cup C_{\varepsilon_{1}}}\mathbf{u}\cdot \mathrm{d}r\qquad g(p)=\int_{C_p}\mathbf{u}\cdot \mathrm{d}r
$$
$$
\implies g(p+\varepsilon \mathbf{e}_1)-g(p)=\int_{C_{\varepsilon_{1}}}\mathbf{u}\cdot \mathrm{d}r
$$

Now I can choose the parametrization $\gamma:[0,\varepsilon]\rightarrow C_{\varepsilon_{1}}$, $t\mapsto p+t\mathbf{e}_1$

$$
\begin{align*}
    \implies \int_{C_{\varepsilon_{1}}}\mathbf{u}\cdot \mathrm{d}r&=\int_{0}^{\varepsilon} \mathbf{u}(p+t\mathbf{e}_1)\cdot \mathbf{e}_1 \ \mathrm{d}t\\
    &=\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\big(u_1(p+t\mathbf{e}_1), u_2(p+t\mathbf{e}_1) \big)\cdot (1,0)\ \mathrm{d}t\\
    &=\int_{0}^{\varepsilon} u_1(p+t\mathbf{e}_1)\ \mathrm{d}t
\end{align*}
$$
Now, we get

$$
\partial_{x}g(p)=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{g(p+\varepsilon \mathbf{e}_1)-g(p)}{\varepsilon}=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{\int_{0}^{\varepsilon} u_1(p+t\mathbf{e}_1)\ \mathrm{d}t}{\varepsilon}
$$

Notice that, letting $G(\varepsilon)\coloneqq \int_{0}^{\varepsilon} u_1(p+t\mathbf{e}_1)\ \mathrm{d}t$, by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon }G(\varepsilon)=u_1(p+\varepsilon \mathbf{e}_1)
$$

Since $G(0)=0$, $\partial_{x}g(p)=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{G(\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}=G^{\prime}(0)=u_1(p)$

Analogously we can also get:

$$
\partial_{y}g(p)=u_2(p) \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$
