## Functions of Several Variables

:::tip Functions of Several Variables

Functions of several variables is: 

$$
f: \mathbb{R}^n\rightarrow \mathbb{R}
$$

More generally, the domain $D$ could just be a subset of $\mathbb{R}$ as:

$$
f:D\rightarrow \mathbb{R}, \qquad D\subseteq \mathbb{R}^n
$$


:::

### Graph of a Function

Graph is a really convinient way to think of a function of several variables, here we will do a example:

#### Paraboloid

:::info Example (Paraboloid)

Consider the function:

$$
f(x,y)=x^2+y^2
$$

this is a case where $n=2$, and we can try to graph it:

$$
\operatorname{graph}(f)= \{(x,y,z)\in \mathbb{R}^3 \text{ such that } z= f(x,y) \}
$$

### Vertical Slice

But, given a graph, how can we determine whether or not it is a paraboloid? Vertical Slice is a good method.

::::tip Vertical Slice

I can slice the graph with vertical plane that contains $z$-axis and the normal direction is on the $x,y$ plane; then, let the normal vector be $a,b,0$, the plane could be written as:

$$
ax+by=0  
\begin{cases}
y=-\frac{a}{b}x\qquad \text{if }b\ne 0\\
x=-\frac{b}{a}y\qquad \text{if }a\ne 0
\end{cases}
$$

then, the equation of the function on such plane looks like:

### Level Sets

::::tip Level Sets

Given a function: 

$$
f:\mathbb{R}^n\rightarrow \mathbb{R}
$$

a **level set** for $f$ is:

$$
\ell_{k}=\{ \mathbf{x}\in \mathbb{R}^n \text{ such that } f(\mathbf{x})=k \}
$$


:::info Example

Back to the example [paraboloid](#paraboloid),

$$
f(x,y)=x^2+y^2
$$
$$
\ell_{k}=\{ \mathbf{x}\in \mathbb{R}^2 \text{ such that } x^2+y^2=k \}
$$

## Continuity and Differentiation of Functions of Several Variables

### Continuity

::::tip Continuity

Our working hypothesis will be that $f$ is continuous, that is:

$$
\lim_{\mathbf{x}\rightarrow \mathbf{x}_0}f(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x}_0) \text{ for any }\mathbf{x}_0 \text{ in the domain}
$$

:::note  

In more precise math terms it means that for any $\varepsilon >0$ there exists a small ball $B$ around $\mathbf{x}_0$ in the domain such that 

$$
f(B)\subseteq \big(f(\mathbf{x}_0)-\varepsilon,\, f(\mathbf{x}_0)+\varepsilon\big)
$$

:::

::::

### Differentiability

:::::tip Differentiability

Given a function $f:\mathbb{R}^n\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$, the partial derivative is defined as:

$$
\partial_{x_i}f(\mathbf{v})=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{f(\mathbf{v}+\varepsilon \mathbf{e}_i)-f(\mathbf{v})}{\varepsilon}
$$

:::danger Working Hypothesis

These partial derivatives exist and are continuous, which means they are not part of the definition, but are assumptions we make for later discussion.

:::

::::info Example

$$
f(x,y)=ye^{x}
$$
$$
\partial_{x}f(x,y)=ye^x\qquad \partial_{y}f(x,y)=e^x
$$
:::note

using our definition, you can check that $\partial_{x}f(x,y)=g^\prime(x)$ where $g(x)=f(x,y)$, treating $y$ as a constant
:::

<hr/>

$$
f(x,y,z)=e^{x^2+y^2-z}
$$
$$
\partial_{x}f=(2x)e^{x^2+y^2-z}\qquad \partial_{y}f=(2y)e^{x^2+y^2-z} \qquad \partial_{z}f=-e^{x^2+y^2-z} 
$$

:::tip Fact (Non-Trivial)
$$
\partial_{y}\partial_{x}f=\partial_{x}\partial_{y}f \text{ if }f\text{ is "nice enough"}
$$
this is "non-trivial", which means we will prove this in a few weeks (link tbd)
:::

<hr/>

If I have $f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$, then:

$$
\begin{align*}
  \partial_{x}f(\mathbf{x}_0)&=\lim_{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0}\frac{f(\mathbf{x}_0+\varepsilon\mathbf{e}_1)-f(\mathbf{x}_0)}{\varepsilon}\\
  &=\lim_{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0}\frac{f(\mathbf{x}_0+\varepsilon)-f(\mathbf{x}_0)}{\varepsilon}\\
  &=f^\prime (\mathbf{x}_0)
\end{align*}
$$
:::danger Remark

$\partial_{x_i}f(\mathbf{x}_0)$ is the derivative at $t=0$ of the function $\mathbb{R}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}: f(\mathbf{x}_0+t\mathbf{e}_i)$

:::

::::

### Gradient

::::tip Gradient

The **Gradient** of $f$, denoted by $\nabla f$, is the vector of partial derivatives
$$
\nabla f(\mathbf{x})=\big(\partial_{x_1}f(\mathbf{x}),\partial_{x_2}f(\mathbf{x}),\dots,\partial_{x_n}f(\mathbf{x})\big)
$$
:::info Example

$$
f(x,y)=x^2-5y^2
$$

$$
\nabla f(x,y)=(2x,-10y)
$$

:::

:::tip Chain Rule

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}f\big(\gamma(t)\big)=\nabla f\big(\gamma(t) \big)\cdot \gamma^{\prime}(t)\quad  \text{where } \gamma \text{ is a curve } \gamma :\mathbb{R}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n
$$

:::

<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

Let's say that $\gamma(t)$ is a level curve for $f$, then:

$$
f\big(\gamma(t) \big)=k \leftarrow \text{(a constant)}
$$

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}f\big(\gamma(t) \big)=\nabla f\big(\gamma(t) \big)\cdot \gamma^{\prime}(t)=0
$$
$$
\implies \nabla f\big(\gamma(t) \big) \perp \gamma^{\prime}(t) \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$

## Linear Approximation of a Function and Tangent Plane

::::tip Linear Approximation 

$$
\begin{align*}
f(x+\varepsilon_1, y+\varepsilon_2, z+\varepsilon_3)&\approx f(x,y,z)
+\varepsilon_1\partial_{x}f(x,y,z)
+\varepsilon_2\partial_{y}f(x,y,z)
+\varepsilon_3\partial_{z}f(x,y,z)\\
&=f(x,y,z)+(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2,\varepsilon_3)\cdot \nabla f(x,y,z)

\end{align*}
$$
More precisely, we are applying that:

$$
\lim_{\|\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2,\varepsilon_3\|\rightarrow 0}\frac{f(x+\varepsilon_1,y+\varepsilon_2,z+\varepsilon_3)-f(x,y,z)-(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2,\varepsilon_3)\cdot \nabla f(x,y,z)}{\|\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2,\varepsilon_3 \|}=0
$$

:::info Example

The tangent plane to the graph of $f$ for a function $f:\mathbb{R}^2
\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$

In particular, for $\mathbf{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon_1,0)$, we get the tangent line:

$$
\big(x,y,f(x,y)\big)+t_1(1,0,\partial_{x}f)
$$

for $\mathbf{\varepsilon}=(0, \varepsilon_2)$, we get the tangent line:

$$
\big(x,y,f(x,y)\big)+t_2(0,1,\partial_{y}f)
$$

Therefore, with the paramatric form of a plane, the tanget plane is:

$$
\big(x,y,f(x,y) \big)+t_1(1,0,\partial_{x}f)+t_2(0,1,\partial_{y}f)
$$
