::::info Notation

We will write $\mathbf{r}(t)$ for the position vector of the particle, where $t$ represents the time.

$\mathbf{r}(t)$ can be seen as a parametrized curve.

### Circular Motion

:::info Circular Motion

Suppose that curve described by the particle of mass $m$ is：

$$
\begin{align*}
    \mathbf{r}_1(t)&=\big(\cos{(t)},\sin{(t)}\big)\\
    \mathbf{r}_2(t)&=\big(\cos{(t)},\sin{(t)},t\big)
\end{align*}
$$

<hr />

1. Compute the force acting on the particle: 

By Newton's Law $\mathbf{F}(t)=m\mathbf{a}(t)$ in curve $\mathbf{r}_1$ we get:

$$
\begin{align*}
    \mathbf{v}_1(t)&=\mathbf{r}^{\prime }(t)=\big(-\sin{(t)},\cos{(t)}\big)\\
    \mathbf{a}_v(t)&=\mathbf{r}^{\prime \prime}=\big(-\cos{(t)},-\sin{(t)}\big)\\
    \implies \mathbf{F}(t)&=m\mathbf{a}(t)=m\big(-\cos{(t)},-\sin{ (t)}\big)
\end{align*}
$$
<hr />

2. Compute $\mathbf{v}_i(t) \cdot \mathbf{F}_i(t)$ and give an explaination for the value:

$$
\begin{align*}
    \mathbf{v}_i(t) \cdot \mathbf{F}_i(t)&=0\\
    \implies \mathbf{v}_i(t) \cdot \mathbf{a}_i(t)&=0\\
\end{align*}
$$
$$
\implies \|\mathbf{v}(t)\| \text{ is constand}
$$
Below is the proof:

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

$$
\begin{align*}
    \left(\|\mathbf{v}(t)\|^2\right)^{\prime}&=\big(\mathbf{v}(t) \cdot \mathbf{v}(t)\big)^\prime\\
                                             &=\big(\mathbf{v}^{\prime}(t) \cdot \mathbf{v}(t)\big)+\big(\mathbf{v}(t) \cdot \mathbf{v}^{\prime}(t)\big) &\qquad \text{(Dot Product Rule)}\\
                                             &=\big(\mathbf{a}(t)\cdot \mathbf{v}(t) \big)+\big(\mathbf{v}(t)\cdot \mathbf{a}(t) \big)\\
                                             &=2\big(\mathbf{v}(t)\cdot \mathbf{a}(t) \big)\\
                                             &=0
\end{align*}
$$
Therefore, $\|\mathbf{v}(t)\|$ is constand. 
<div style={{ textAlign: 'right' }}>
  $\blacksquare$
</div>
</details>
:::

### Angular Momentum

::::tip Angular Momentum

For a particle moving along a curve, its angular momentum $\mathbf{M}(t)$ is:

$$
\mathbf{M}(t)=m\big( \mathbf{r}(t)\times \mathbf{v}(t)\big)
$$
:::note Note
The Angular Momentum measures how much one particle is rotating with aspect to the origin.
:::

In the case of $\mathbf{r}(t)=\big(\cos{(t)},\sin{(t)}，0 \big)$, both $\mathbf{r}(t)$ and $\mathbf{v}(t)$ lie on $xy$ plane; therefore, $\mathbf{M}(t)$ lies along $z$ axis. 
$$
\mathbf{r}(t)=\big(\cos{(t)},\sin{(t)},0 \big) \qquad \mathbf{v}(t)=\big(-\sin{(t)},\cos{(t)},0 \big)
$$
$$
\mathbf{M}(t)=m\mathbf{r}(t)\times\mathbf{v}(t)=m(0,0,1)
$$

::::


### Torque

::::tip Torque

When take the derivative of angular momentum, we have:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{M}(t)=\mathbf{M}^{\prime}(t)=\mathbf{r}(t)\times \mathbf{F}(t)
$$
where $\mathbf{r}(t)\times \mathbf{F}(t)$ is called the **Torque**

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

$$
\begin{align*}
    \mathbf{M}(t)&=m\mathbf{r}(t)\times \mathbf{v}(t)\\
    \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{M}(t)&=m\big(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}(t)\times \mathbf{v}(t) +\mathbf{r}(t)\times \mathbf{v}^{\prime}(t)\big) &\qquad \text{(Cross Product Rule)}\\
                                               &=m\big(\mathbf{v}\times \mathbf{v}(t) +\mathbf{r}(t)\times \mathbf{a}(t)\big)\\
                                               &=m\big(\mathbf{r}(t)\times \mathbf{a}(t)\big) & \qquad \big(\mathbf{v}\times \mathbf{v}(t)=0\big)\\
                                               &=\mathbf{r}(t)\times\mathbf{F}(t) &\qquad \text{(Inverse Constant Scalar Multiple)} \tag*{$\blacksquare$}

\end{align*}
$$

</details>

:::note Remarkable Particular Case

From the definition above, if the torque is $0$, such that $\mathbf{r}(t)\times \mathbf{F}(t)=0$, then:

$$
\mathbf{M}(t) \text{ is constant}
$$

This will lead to another remarkable consequence that we will introduce below.
:::

::::

### Kepler's Law

:::tip Kepler's Law

If $\mathbf{M}(t)$ is constant, then $\mathbf{r}(t)$ is a planar; that is to say, $\mathbf{r}(t)$ lies on a plane.

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

Let's say that if we suspect that this is true, then $\mathbf{M}(t)=\mathbf{M}$ should be the normal direction of the plane. Therefore, our conjecture become 

$$
\mathbf{r}(t)\cdot \mathbf{M}(t)=\text{constant}
$$
Let's compute $\big(\mathbf{r}(t)\cdot \mathbf{M}\big)^{\prime}$:

$$
\big(\mathbf{r}(t)\cdot \mathbf{M(t)}\big)^{\prime}=\Bigr(\mathbf{r}(t)\cdot\big(\underbrace{\mathbf{r}(t)\times \mathbf{v}(t)}_{\text{is orthogonal to } \mathbf{r}(t) \text{ and } \mathbf{v}(t)} \big) \Bigr)=0
$$
$$
\implies \text{the curve lies on a plane with normal direction } \mathbf{M} \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$
</details>
:::

### Purely Math Statement

:::tip Purely Math Statement

Consider a curve $\gamma(t)\times \gamma^{\prime}(t)$ is constant, then the trajectory is plane; that is to said, $\gamma(t)$ lies on a plane.

:::
