## Curves

A (parametrized) curve is a function: 

$$
\gamma: \mathbb{R}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^3
$$

and our working assumptionis that we can take the derivative of $\gamma$.

- A curve $\gamma{(s)}$ is arclength parametrized if $\|\gamma^{\prime}(s)\|=1$ for any $s$, that is, we are going along the curve at constant speed $1$.

- How to compute the arclength parametrization? Set:

    $$
    s(t)=\int_{t_0}^{t}\|\gamma^{\prime}(u) \|\mathrm{d}u
    $$
    and invert the function to get $t(s)$

:::info Important Case

If $\gamma^{\prime}(t)=c$ as a constant, then $s(t)=ct$ and $t(s)=\frac{1}{c}s$ 

:::

<hr/>

Now, assume $\gamma(s)$ is arclength parametrized curve:

### Unit Tangent Vector
:::tip Unit Tangent Vector
$$
\mathbf{T}(s)\coloneqq\gamma^{\prime}(s),\qquad \|\mathbf{T}(s) \|=\|\gamma^{\prime}(s) \|=1
$$
:::
### Curvature
:::tip Curvature
$$
\kappa(s)\coloneqq \left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s} \mathbf{T}(s) \right\|
$$

it measures how much the curve is "curving"; $\kappa=0$ if and only if $\gamma(s)$ is a line; if $\gamma(s)$ is a circle of radius $R$, then $\kappa(s)=\frac{1}{R}$
:::


### Unit Normal Vector

:::tip Unit Normal Vector
If $\kappa(s)\ne 0$

$$
\mathbf{N}(s)\coloneqq \frac{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\mathbf{T}(s)}{\kappa(s)},\qquad \|\mathbf{N}(s)\|=1
$$
:::

### Unit Binormal Vector


:::tip Unit Binormal Vector
If $\kappa(s)\ne 0$

$$
\mathbf{B}(s)\coloneqq \mathbf{T}(s)\times\mathbf{N}(s)
$$
:::

### Torsion

:::tip Torsion


$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\mathbf{B}(s)$ is proportional to $\mathrm{N}(s)$

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\mathbf{B}(s)=-\tau(s) \mathrm{N}(s)
$$
$\tau(s)$ is called torsion, it s a scalar function measures how much the curve is "twisting", and $\tau(s)=-\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\mathbf{B}(s)\cdot \mathbf{N}(s) \right)$
:::

### Frernet Formulas
::::tip Frernet Formulas
$$
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\mathbf{T}(s)&= \kappa(s)\mathbf{N}(s)\\
    \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\mathbf{N}(s)&= -\kappa(s)\mathbf{T}(s)+\tau(s)\mathbf{B}(s)\\
    \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\mathbf{B}(s)&= -\tau(s)\mathbf{N}(s)\\
\end{align*}
$$

## Linear Algebra

### Vector Space

:::tip Vector Space

A vector space is a set together with 2 operations, addition $(V\times V\rightarrow V)$ and scalar multiplication $(\mathbb{R}\times V\rightarrow V)$ that satisfying a bunch of properties.

:::

### Linearly Independent Vectors

:::tip Linearly Independent Vectors

A set of vectors $\{\mathbf{v}_1,\dots ,\mathbf{v}_k \}$ is linearly independent if:

$$
c_1\mathbf{v}_1+ \dots+ c_k\mathbf{v}_k= 0 \implies c_i= 0\text{ for any } i
$$

:::

### System fo Generator

:::tip System fo Generator

A set of vectors $\{\mathbf{v}_1,\dots ,\mathbf{v}_k \}$ is a system of generator if any vector $\mathbf{v}\in V$ can be writtten as:

$$
\mathbf{v}=c_1\mathbf{v}_1+ \dots+ c_k\mathbf{v}_k \text{ for some } c_i\in V
$$
:::

### Basis

::::tip Basis

A set of vectors $\{\mathbf{v}_1,\dots ,\mathbf{v}_k \}$ is a basis for $V$ if it is both linearly independent and a system of generator

:::note Fact

All basis of a vector space have the same number of elements

:::
::::

### Dimension

::::tip Dimension

The dimension of a vector space is the number of elements of a basis


:::info Example

$$
\operatorname{dim} \mathbb{R}^n=n \text{ a basis is given by } \{\mathbf{e}_1,\dots, \mathbf{e}_n\}
$$

:::
::::

### Linear Systems

:::tip Linear Systems

Linear systems help us find the solution set to:

$$
\begin{cases}
a_{11}{x_1}+\dots+ a_{1n}{x}_n=b_1\\
\quad \qquad \vdots \quad \qquad \vdots\\
a_{m1}{x_1}+\dots+ a_{mn}{x}_n=b_m\\
\end{cases}
$$

the solution set is a vector space if and only if $\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{0}$ (a nonempty subset of a vector space is vector space if it is closed under addition and multiple by scalar), and we get the coefficient matrix:
$$
\left[
\begin{array}{ccc|c}
a_{11} & \cdots & a_{1n} & b_1 \\
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\
a_{m1} & \cdots & a_{mn} & b_m
\end{array}
\right]
$$

by performing elementary [row operations](docs\Notes\Stony-Brook\MAT307\9-17-2026.mdx#elementary-row-operations), we can reduce the matrix to the echlon form:

$$
\left[
\begin{array}{ccccccccc|c}
0 & \boxed{1} & * & * & 0 & * & * & 0 & * & \\
1 & 0 & * & * & \boxed{1} & * & * & 0 & * & \\
1 & 1 & * & * & 0 & * & * & \boxed{1} & * & \mathbf{b} \\
0 & 0 & \cdots & \cdots & 1 & \cdots & \cdots & 0 & \cdots & \\
\vdots & \vdots & & & 0 & & & 0 & &
\end{array}
\right]
$$

where $\boxed{1}$ is the pivot.
:::

### Rank

:::tip rank

Rank of the matrix is the number of pivots

:::

### Solution Sets

::::tip Solution Sets

The solution set is non empty if and only if $\operatorname{rank}({A}\mid \mathbf{b})=\operatorname{rank}(A)$

Additionally, the soulution set can be parametrized from the echelon forms setting as parameters $t_1=x_{k_i}$, where $k_1$ is a non-pivot solumn
A

Moreover, the dimension of the solution set:

$$
\operatorname{dim} S_0=n-\operatorname{rank}(A)
$$
which is quite intuitive because the number of variable in the solution set is the number of free column in the matrix $A$.

### Level Set

::::tip Level Set

A level set $\ell_k$ is the set of points in $\mathbb{R}^n$ where $f$ takes value $k$, such as:

$$
\ell_k\coloneqq \{\mathbf{x}\in \mathbb{R}^n \text{ such that }f(\mathbf{x})=k \}
$$
:::info Example

If $f(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2 $, then:

$$
\ell_k=\{x^2+y^@2z^2=k \}
$$
is a sphere of radius $\sqrt{k}$
:::
:::danger Remark

To check whether a curve $\gamma$ lies on a level set for $f$, I need to check whether $f\big(\gamma(t) \big)$ is constant (or I can show a picture of the curve and of the ground level set and argue geometrically).

:::

### Gradient 

::::tip Gradient 

The gradient is a vector in every point of the domain $f$, and:
$$
\nabla f(x,y)=(\partial_{x}f,\partial_y{f})
$$

geometrocaly, it gives us the direction of maximal increase.

::::

### Taylor Expansion (2nd Case)

:::tip Taylor Expansion (2nd Case)

$$
\begin{align*}
f\big((x,y)+(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2)\big)
&= f(x,y)
+ \varepsilon_1 \partial_x f(x,y)
+ \varepsilon_2 \partial_y f(x,y) \\
&\quad + \frac{\varepsilon_1^2}{2}\partial_x^2 f(x,y)
+ \varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2\partial_x\partial_y f(x,y) \\
&\quad + \frac{\varepsilon_2^2}{2}\partial_y^2 f(x,y)
+ \text{terms of order } |\varepsilon|^3.
\end{align*}
$$

I am thinking of this as a function of $\mathbf{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2$ where $\mathbf{\varepsilon}$ is a small displacement vector around $(x,y)$
- It gives us the best local polynomial of degree $n$ approximation of $f$, as all its derivatives agree with the ones of $f$ at $(x,y)$

:::

### Chain Rule

:::tip Chain Rule

If $\gamma:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$ is a curve and $f:\mathbb{R}^n\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is a multivariable function, then:
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}f\big(\gamma(t) \big)=\nabla f\big(f(\gamma(t))\big)\cdot \gamma(t)
$$

### Min and Max

:::tip Min and Max

$f:D\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is a function, then I have a small ball around each point, which means there is no boundary. Moreally, I wat to be able to move around each point in any possible direction. 

- If $p_0\in D$ is local mind or local max, then $\nabla f(p_0)=0$

- If $\nabla f(p_0)=0$, then the Hessian criterion may decide whether $p_0$ is local min/max.
$$
\operatorname{Hess}(f)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\partial_x^2 f(p) & \partial_x\partial_y f(p)\\
\partial_x\partial_y f(p) & \partial_y^2 f(p)
\end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{Hessian determinant} & \text{Additional condition} & \text{Conclusion} \\ \hline
\det(H)>0 & f_{xx}>0 & \text{Local minimum} \\
\det(H)>0 & f_{xx}<0 & \text{Local maximum} \\
\det(H)<0 & \text{None} & \text{Saddle point} \\
\det(H)=0 & \text{None} & \text{Inconclusive}
\end{array}
$$


:::
