Functions​

Recap
  • Injective x1,x2∈X, x1≠x2  ⟹  f(x1)≠f(x2)x_1,x_2\in X, \ x_1\ne x_2\implies f(x_1)\ne f(x_2)x1​,x2​∈X, x1​=x2​⟹f(x1​)=f(x2​)
  • Surjective ∀y∈Y,∃x∈X such that f(x)=y\forall y\in Y,\exists x\in X\text{ such that }f(x)=y∀y∈Y,∃x∈X such that f(x)=y
  • Bijective ∀y∈Y,∃!x∈X such that f(x)=y\forall y\in Y, \exists!x\in X\text{ such that }f(x)=y∀y∈Y,∃!x∈X such that f(x)=y

  • Invertible A function f:X→Yf:X\rightarrow Yf:X→Y is invertible if ∃g:Y→X\exists g:Y\rightarrow X∃g:Y→X such that:

    f∘g=id⁡Yandg∘f=id⁡Xf\circ g=\operatorname{id}_Y\qquad \text{and}\qquad g\circ f=\operatorname{id}_Xf∘g=idY​andg∘f=idX​
Theorem

A function f:X→Yf:X\rightarrow Yf:X→Y is bijective if and only if it is invertible.

Derivation

  1.   ⟹  \implies ⟹ Direction: from bijection to invertible

    Define:

    g:Y⟶X, y∈Y, g(y)=xg:Y\longrightarrow X,\ y\in Y, \ g(y)=xg:Y⟶X, y∈Y, g(y)=x g(f(x))=g(y)=x and f(g(y))=f(x)=y  ⟹  g∘f=id⁡X and f∘g=id⁡Yg\big(f(x)\big)=g(y)=x\text{ and }f\big(g(y)\big)=f(x)=y\implies g\circ f=\operatorname{id}_X\text{ and }f\circ g=\operatorname{id}_Yg(f(x))=g(y)=x and f(g(y))=f(x)=y⟹g∘f=idX​ and f∘g=idY​

  2. ⟸\Longleftarrow ⟸ Direction: from invertible to bijection

    ∃g:Y→X such that g∘f=id⁡X and f∘g=id⁡Y\exists g:Y\rightarrow X \text{ such that } g\circ f=\operatorname{id}_X\text{ and }f\circ g=\operatorname{id}_Y∃g:Y→X such that g∘f=idX​ and f∘g=idY​

We show fff is both injective and surjective:

Injective

Prove the contrapositive f(x1)=f(x2)  ⟹  x1=x2f(x_1)=f(x_2)\implies x_1=x_2f(x1​)=f(x2​)⟹x1​=x2​:

x1,x2∈X, f(x1)=f(x2)g(f(x1))=g(f(x2))(g∘f)(x1)=(g∘f)(x2)id⁡X(x1)=id⁡X(x2)x1=x2\begin{align*} x_1,x_2\in X,\ f(x_1)&=f(x_2)\\ g\big(f(x_1) \big)&=g\big(f(x_2) \big)\\ (g \circ f)(x_1)&=(g \circ f)(x_2)\\ \operatorname{id}_X(x_1)&=\operatorname{id}_X(x_2)\\ x_1&=x_2 \end{align*}x1​,x2​∈X, f(x1​)g(f(x1​))(g∘f)(x1​)idX​(x1​)x1​​=f(x2​)=g(f(x2​))=(g∘f)(x2​)=idX​(x2​)=x2​​

Surjective

For all y∈Yy\in Yy∈Y, let x=g(y)x=g(y)x=g(y):

f(x)=f(g(y))=(f∘g)(y)=id⁡Y(y)=y\begin{align*} f(x)&=f\big(g(y)\big)\\ &=(f\circ g)(y)\\ &=\operatorname{id}_Y(y)\\ &=y \end{align*}f(x)​=f(g(y))=(f∘g)(y)=idY​(y)=y​

Therefore, g∘f=id⁡Xg\circ f=\operatorname{id}_Xg∘f=idX​ and f∘g=id⁡Yf\circ g=\operatorname{id}_Yf∘g=idY​, so fff is bijective.

■\blacksquare■

Moreover, if fff is invertible, then its inverse is unique.

Derivation

Let g1:Y→Xg_1: Y\rightarrow Xg1​:Y→X and g2:Y→Xg_2: Y\rightarrow Xg2​:Y→X.

Here, we have:

f∘g1=id⁡Yg1∘f=id⁡Xf∘g2=id⁡Yg2∘f=id⁡Xf\circ g_1=\operatorname{id}_{Y}\quad g_1\circ f=\operatorname{id}_{X}\quad f\circ g_2=\operatorname{id}_{Y}\quad g_2\circ f=\operatorname{id}_{X}f∘g1​=idY​g1​∘f=idX​f∘g2​=idY​g2​∘f=idX​

And we want to show:

g1(y)=g2(y)∀y∈Yg_1(y)=g_2(y) \quad \forall y\in Yg1​(y)=g2​(y)∀y∈Y

Let y∈Yy\in Yy∈Y, then:

{g1(y)=x1g2(y)=x2  ⟹  {f(g1(y))=f(x1)f(g2(y))=f(x2)  ⟹  {id⁡Y(y)=f(x1)id⁡Y(y)=f(x2)  ⟹  f(x1)=f(x2)\begin{cases} g_1(y)=x_1\\ g_2(y)=x_2 \end{cases} \implies \begin{cases} f\big(g_1(y) \big)=f(x_1)\\ f\big(g_2(y) \big)=f(x_2) \end{cases} \implies \begin{cases} \operatorname{id}_{Y}(y)=f(x_1)\\ \operatorname{id}_{Y}(y)=f(x_2) \end{cases} \implies f(x_1)=f(x_2){g1​(y)=x1​g2​(y)=x2​​⟹{f(g1​(y))=f(x1​)f(g2​(y))=f(x2​)​⟹{idY​(y)=f(x1​)idY​(y)=f(x2​)​⟹f(x1​)=f(x2​)  ⟹  x1=x2  ⟹  g1=g2  ⟹  g is unique   ⟹  g=f−1\implies x_1=x_2\implies g_1=g_2\implies g \text{ is unique }\implies g=f^{-1}⟹x1​=x2​⟹g1​=g2​⟹g is unique ⟹g=f−1

■\blacksquare■
