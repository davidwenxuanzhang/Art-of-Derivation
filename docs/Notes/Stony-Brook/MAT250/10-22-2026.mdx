## Review

Recall from last time

:::note Notation

For a positive integer $n$, we define $\mathbb{N}_n\coloneqq \{1,2,\dots,n\}$

:::
:::tip Definitions

- Cardinality:

    A non-empty set $X$ has cardinality $n$ if there exists a bijection 

    $$
    f:\mathbb{N}_n\longrightarrow X
    $$

    We then write the cardinality as $|X|\coloneqq n$.

<hr/>

- Finite Set

    A set is finite if it has cardinality $n$ for some integer $n\ge 0$.

<hr/>

- Infinite Set

    A set is said to be infinite if it is not finite.   
<hr/>

- Lemma

    If there exists an injection $f:\mathbb{N}_m\rightarrow \mathbb{N}_n$, then $m\le n$
:::

### Uniqueness of Finite Cardinality

:::tip Uniqueness of Finite Cardinality

Suppose

$$
f:\mathbb{N}_m\longrightarrow X\quad \text{ and }\quad g:\mathbb{N}_n\longrightarrow X
$$

is a bijection, then $m=n$. 

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

Since $f$ and $g$ are bijections, their inverses exist, so the map:

$$
g^{-1}\circ f: \mathbb{N}_m\longrightarrow \mathbb{N}_n
$$
is injective, so the [Lemma](docs/Notes/Stony-Brook/MAT250/10-20-2026.mdx#finite-set-and-infinite-set) gives $m\le n$. Similarly

$$
f^{-1}\circ g: \mathbb{N}_n\longrightarrow \mathbb{N}_m
$$

is injective, so by the [Lemma](docs/Notes/Stony-Brook/MAT250/10-20-2026.mdx#finite-set-and-infinite-set), $n\le m$

Therefore, $m=n$.

</details>

:::

:::tip Lemma

Let $X$ and $Y$ be nonempty finite sets. If there exists an injection $X\rightarrow Y$, then $|X|\le |Y|$

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

We write $|X|=m$ and $|Y|=n$, and choose bijection:

$$
g:\mathbb{N}_m\longrightarrow X,\qquad h:\mathbb{N}_n\longrightarrow Y
$$

Then, 

$$
h^{-1}\circ f\circ g:\mathbb{N}_m\longrightarrow \mathbb{N}_n
$$
is injective, and by the [Lemma](docs/Notes/Stony-Brook/MAT250/10-20-2026.mdx#finite-set-and-infinite-set), we get $m\le n$.

<div style={{ textAlign: 'right' }}>
  $\blacksquare$
</div>

</details>

:::

### The Pigeonhole Principle

:::tip The Pigeonhole Principle

Let $f:X\rightarrow Y$ be a function between nonempty finite sets. If
$$
|X|>|Y|
$$
then $f$ is not injective. Equivalently, there exist distinct elements $x_1,x_2\in X$ such that $f(x_1)=f(x_2)$

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

This is the contrapositive of the previous lemma.

<div style={{ textAlign: 'right' }}>
  $\blacksquare$
</div>

</details>

:::

## The Addition Principle

:::tip Addition Principle

If $X$ and $Y$ are disjoint finite sets, then $X\cup Y$ is finite and 

$$
|X\cup Y|=|X|+|Y|
$$

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

Let $|X|=n$ and $|Y|=m$, and choose bijection
$$
f:\mathbb{N}_n\longrightarrow X, \qquad g:\mathbb{N}_m\longrightarrow Y
$$
Define 
$$
h:\mathbb{N}_{n+m}\longrightarrow X\cup Y
$$
Now, we just need to show that $h$ is bijective, which is both surjective and injective. 

- Surjective:
    as notice that
    $$
    h(t)=\begin{cases}
    f(t), &1\le t\le n\\
    g(t-n), &n+1\le t\le n+m
    \end{cases}
    $$
    Thus, $h$ is surjective because both $f$ and $g$ are surjective.
- Injective: recall that for an injective function, given $x_1,x_2\in X$, if $f$ is injective and $f(x_1)=f(x_2)$, then $x_1=x_2$.
    Similarly, suppose $h(i)=h(j)$ where $i,j\in \mathbb{N}_{n+m}$
    - Case 1: If $i,j\le n$, then $h(i)=h(j)\implies f(i)=f(j)$, and the injectivity of $f$ gives $i=j$
    - Case 2: If $i,j\ge n+1$, then $h(i)=h(j)\implies g(i-n)=g(j-n)$, and the injectivity of $g$ gives $i-n=j-n\implies i=j$
    - Case 3: If one of the $i,j$ is at most $n$ and the other is greater than $n$, then assume $i\le n$ and $j> n $, we have $h(i)=f(i)\in X$ and $h(j)=g(j-n)\in Y$; since $X\cap Y= \emptyset$, this case cannot happen.
    Thus, $h$ is injective.
Therefore, $h$ is bijective, and according to the [definition of Cardinality](docs/Notes/Stony-Brook/MAT250/10-20-2026.mdx#cardinality):

$$
|X\cup Y|=n+m=|X|+|Y|\tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$
</details>

:::

:::tip Theorem

Let $X_1,\dots, X_n$ be pairwise disjoint finite sets, then $\cup_{i=1}^{n}X_i$ is finite, and:

$$
\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}X_i \right|= \sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_i|
$$

:::

## The Multiplication Principle

::::tip Multiplication Principle

If $X$ and $Y$ are finite sets, then their [Cartesian Product](docs/Notes/Stony-Brook/MAT250/9-29-2026.mdx#cartesian-product) $X\times Y$ is finite and $|X\times Y|=|X||Y|$

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

If $X$ or $Y$ is empty, then $X\times Y$ is empty, and the result follows. 

Otherwise, we write: 

$$
X=\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}
$$

Then from the [Proposition](docs/Notes/Stony-Brook/MAT250/10-1-2026.mdx#set-operations) we learned previously, 
$$
X\times Y=\big(\{x_1\}\times Y\big)\cup \dots\cup \big(\{x_n\}\times Y\big)
$$
These sets are pairwise disjoint, and each has $|Y|$ elements, therefore by [Addition Principle](#the-addition-principle)

$$
\begin{align*}
|X\times Y|&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big|\{x_i \}\times Y \big|\\
&=\underbrace{|Y|+\dots+|Y|}_{n \text{ terms}}\\
&=n|Y|\\
&= |X||Y| \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
\end{align*} 
$$

</details>

:::info Example

Let 
$$
X=\{(x,y)\in \mathbb{N}_9\times\mathbb{N}_9\mid x\ne y \}
$$

The product $\mathbb{N}_9\times\mathbb{N}_9$ is the disjoint union of $X$ and the diagonal 

$$
\{(x,y)\in \mathbb{N}_9\times\mathbb{N}_9\mid x= y \}
$$
which has 9 elements, hence:
$$
81=|X|+9\implies |X|=72
$$

:::

## Inclusion-Exclusion 

### Inclusion-Exclusion for Two Sets

:::tip Inclusion-Exclusion for Two Sets

If $X$ and $Y$ are finite sets, then 

$$
|X\cup Y|=|X|+|Y|-|X\cap Y|
$$

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

Recall from the [Pairwise-Disjoint Decomposition](docs/Notes/Stony-Brook/MAT250/9-24-2026.mdx#set-proposition):

$$
X\cup Y= (X\setminus Y)\cup  (Y\setminus X)\cup (X\cap Y)
$$

Therefore, by the [Addition Principle](#the-addition-principle):

$$
|X\cup Y|= |X\setminus Y|+|Y\setminus X|+|X\cap Y|
$$

Also, because
$$
X=(X\setminus Y)\cup (X\cap Y)
$$
is a disjoint union, so 
$$
|X\setminus Y|= |X|-|X\cap Y|
$$

Similarly, we can have:

$$
|Y\setminus X|= |Y|-|X\cap Y|
$$

Plug in those back:

$$
\begin{align*}
    |X\cup Y|&=\big( |X|-|X\cap Y|\big)+\big( |Y|-|X\cap Y|\big)+|X\cap Y|\\
    &=|X|+|Y|-|X\cap Y| \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
\end{align*}
$$

</details>

:::

### Inclusion-Exclusion for Three Sets

:::tip Inclusion-Exclusion for Three Sets

If $X,Y,Z$ are finite sets, then

$$
|X\cup Y\cup Z|=|X|+|Y|+|Z|-|X\cap Y|-|Y\cap Z|-|X\cap Z|+|X\cap Y\cap Z |
$$

:::

## Finite Sets for the Real Numbers

::::tip Maximum and Minimum Element

Let $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}$. An element $b\in A$ is a **minimum element** of $A$, written $b=\operatorname{min}A$, if $b\le a$ for every $a\in A$

An element $c\in A$ is a **maximum element** of $A$, written $c=\operatorname{max}A$, if $c\ge a$ for every $a\in A$

:::info Example

For 
$$
A=\{-1,17,12,-78,8 \}
$$
we have:

$$
\operatorname{min}A=-78\quad \text{and}\quad \operatorname{max}A=17
$$

:::

:::danger Well-Ordering Principle

Every nonempty set of positive integers has a minimum element. This is the **Well-Ordering Principle**, which is equivalent to the [Induction Principle](docs/Notes/Stony-Brook/MAT250/9-17-2026.mdx#mathematical-induction).

:::

:::info Example

The set $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ has neither a minimum nor maximum element. Given $a\in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$

$$
\frac{a}{2}\in \mathbb{R}_{>0}\quad \text{and}\quad \frac{a}{2}<a
$$
so $a$ cannot be a minimum. Similarly, $2a>a $, so $a$ cannot be a maximum.
:::


::::

:::info Theorem

Every nonempty finite subset of $\mathbb{R}$ has a minimum and maximum element.

:::
