## Functions

::::tip Recap

- Injective
    $$ 
    x_1,x_2\in X, \ x_1\ne x_2\implies f(x_1)\ne f(x_2)
    $$
<hr/>

- Surjective
    $$
    \forall y\in Y,\exists x\in X\text{ such that }f(x)=y
    $$
<hr/>

- Bijective
    $$
    \forall y\in Y, \exists!x\in X\text{ such that }f(x)=y
    $$
<hr/>

- Invertible
    A function $f:X\rightarrow Y$ is invertible if $\exists g:Y\rightarrow X$ such that:

    $$
    f\circ g=\operatorname{id}_Y\qquad \text{and}\qquad g\circ f=\operatorname{id}_X
    $$

:::info Theorem

A function $f:X\rightarrow Y$ is bijective if and only if it is invertible. 

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

1. $\implies $ Direction: from bijection to invertible

    Define:
    $$
    g:Y\longrightarrow X,\ y\in Y, \ g(y)=x
    $$
    $$
    g\big(f(x)\big)=g(y)=x\text{ and }f\big(g(y)\big)=f(x)=y\implies g\circ f=\operatorname{id}_X\text{ and }f\circ g=\operatorname{id}_Y
    $$
2. $\Longleftarrow $ Direction: from invertible to bijection

    $$
    \exists g:Y\rightarrow X \text{ such that } g\circ f=\operatorname{id}_X\text{ and }f\circ g=\operatorname{id}_Y
    $$ 

We show $f$ is both injective and surjective:

**Injective**

Prove the contrapositive $f(x_1)=f(x_2)\implies x_1=x_2$:

$$
\begin{align*}
x_1,x_2\in X,\ f(x_1)&=f(x_2)\\
g\big(f(x_1) \big)&=g\big(f(x_2) \big)\\
(g \circ f)(x_1)&=(g \circ f)(x_2)\\
\operatorname{id}_X(x_1)&=\operatorname{id}_X(x_2)\\
x_1&=x_2
\end{align*}
$$

  </div>

  <div
    style={{
      paddingLeft: "2rem",
    }}
  >

**Surjective**

For all $y\in Y$, let $x=g(y)$:

$$
\begin{align*}
    f(x)&=f\big(g(y)\big)\\
    &=(f\circ g)(y)\\
    &=\operatorname{id}_Y(y)\\
    &=y
\end{align*}
$$

  </div>
</div>

Therefore, $g\circ f=\operatorname{id}_X$ and $f\circ g=\operatorname{id}_Y$, so $f$ is bijective.

<div style={{ textAlign: 'right' }}>
  $\blacksquare$
</div>

</details>

Moreover, if $f$ is invertible, then its inverse is unique.


<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary> 

Let $g_1: Y\rightarrow X$ and $g_2: Y\rightarrow X$.

Here, we have:

$$
f\circ g_1=\operatorname{id}_{Y}\quad g_1\circ f=\operatorname{id}_{X}\quad f\circ g_2=\operatorname{id}_{Y}\quad g_2\circ f=\operatorname{id}_{X}
$$
And we want to show:

$$
g_1(y)=g_2(y) \quad \forall y\in Y
$$

Let $y\in Y$, then:
$$
\begin{cases}
    g_1(y)=x_1\\
    g_2(y)=x_2
\end{cases}
\implies 
\begin{cases}
    f\big(g_1(y) \big)=f(x_1)\\
    f\big(g_2(y) \big)=f(x_2)
\end{cases}
\implies 
\begin{cases}
    \operatorname{id}_{Y}(y)=f(x_1)\\
    \operatorname{id}_{Y}(y)=f(x_2)
\end{cases}
\implies f(x_1)=f(x_2)
$$
$$
\implies x_1=x_2\implies g_1=g_2\implies g \text{ is unique }\implies g=f^{-1}
$$

<div style={{ textAlign: 'right' }}>
  $\blacksquare$
</div>

</details>

:::info Examples

Let

$$
f:\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0 }\longrightarrow \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0 },\qquad f(x)=x^2
$$
The function is injective, as we prove its contrapositive, with $x_1,x_2\in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$, then:

$$
\begin{align*}
f(x_1)=f(x_2)&\implies x_1^2=x_2^2\\
&\implies x_1^2-x_2^2=0\\
&\implies(x_1+x_2)(x_1-x_2)=0\\
&\implies (x_1+x_2)=0\text{ or }(x_1-x_2)=0\\
&\implies x_1=x_2=0\text{ or }x_1=x_2\\
&\implies x_1=x_2
\end{align*}
$$

Therefore, $f$ is injective.

The function is also surjective, as given $y\in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$, the number $\sqrt{y}\in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$ satisfies:

$$
f(\sqrt{y})=y
$$

Therefore, the function is bijective, with:

$$
f^{-1}(y)=\sqrt{y}
$$

<hr/>

Let:

$$
f:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow \mathbb{R},\qquad f(x)=5x+11
$$
The function is injective because

$$
5x_1+11=5x_2+11\implies x_1=x_2
$$
The function is also surjective because, for any $y\in \mathbb{R}$:

$$
f\left(\frac{y-11}{5} \right)=y
$$

Hence, the function is bijective, with:

$$
f^{-1}(y)=\frac{y-11}{5}
$$
:::

### Trigonometric Examples

:::info Sine Function

The function:

$$
\sin : \mathbb{R}\longrightarrow \mathbb{R}
$$
is not injective because:

$$
\sin{0}=\sin{\pi}
$$

it is not surjective because:

$$
\sin{x}\in [-1,1]
$$

However,

$$
\sin: \left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right]\longrightarrow [-1,1] 
$$
is bijective; consequently,

$$
\sin^{-1}:[-1,1]\longrightarrow \left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right]
$$
exists.

:::

:::info Tangent Function

The tangent function on its full natural domain

$$
\tan:\mathbb{R}\setminus \left\{(2n+1)\frac{\pi}{2}\mid n\in \mathbb{Z} \right\}\longrightarrow \mathbb{R}
$$

is surjective but not injective, because $\tan{0}=\tan{\pi}$; but its restriction

$$
\tan: \left(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2} \right)\longrightarrow \mathbb{R}
$$
is bijective, so

$$
\tan^{-1}:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow \left(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2} \right)
$$
exists.
:::

### Dependence on the Domain and Codomain

:::info Example

Consider

$$
f_1:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow \mathbb{R},\qquad f_1(x)=4x^2-4x+2
$$
Completing the square gives:

$$
f_1(x)=(2x-1)^2+1
$$
Therefore $f_1(x)\ge 1$ for all $x$, so no $y< 1$ lies in the image, and thus $f_1$ is not surjective.

$$
f_1(1)=f_1(0)=2
$$
Thus $f_1$ is also not injective, and hence not bijective.

<hr/>

Now, consider:

$$
f_2:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\longrightarrow [1,\infty),\qquad f_2(x)=4x^2-4x+2
$$
For every $y\ge 1$, choose $x=\frac{1+\sqrt{y-1}}{2}$, then $f_2(x)=y$, so $f_2$ is surjective.

$$
f_2\left(\frac{3}{4} \right)=f_2\left(\frac{1}{4} \right)=\frac{5}{4}
$$
Thus $f_2$ is not injective, and therefore not bijective.
:::
