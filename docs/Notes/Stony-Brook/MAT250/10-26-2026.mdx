## Review

Recall from last time:

:::tip Minimum and Maximum Elements

Let $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be a nonempty set. An element $b\in A$ is a **minimum element** of $A$, written $b=\operatorname{min}A$, if $b\le a$ for every $a\in A$

Also, an element $c\in A$ is a **maximum element** of $A$, written $c=\operatorname{max}A$, if $c\ge a$ for every $a\in A$.

:::

:::info Theorem 

Every nonempty finite subset of $\mathbb{R}$ has a minimum and a maximum element.

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

Let $A$ be a nonempty finite subset of $\mathbb{R}$ with $|A|=n$; we proceed by induction on $n$.

- Base Case: when $n=1$, $A=\{a\}$, so by the definition of $\operatorname{min}A$, $\operatorname{min}A$ exists and $\operatorname{min}A=a$

- Inductive Step: Assume the statement holds for $k$: every $k$-element subset of $\mathbb{R}$ has a minimum and a maximum element. Let

    $$
    A=\{a_1,\dots,a_{k+1} \},\qquad A^{\prime}=\{a_1,\dots, a_k\}
    $$
    By the induction hypothesis, $a=\operatorname{min}A^{\prime}$ exists. Then:
    $$
    \operatorname{min}A=
    \begin{cases}
        a_{k+1} , &a_{k+1}\le a\\
        a  , &a_{k+1}>a
    \end{cases}
    $$
    Thus $A$ has a minimum; similarly, $A$ also has a maximum.

<div style={{ textAlign: 'right' }}>
  $\blacksquare$
</div>

</details>

:::

## The Greatest Common Divisor

Recall from [Divisibility](docs/Notes/Stony-Brook/MAT250/9-17-2026.mdx#divisibility), given $a,d\in \mathbb{Z}, d\ne 0$, if

$$
d\mid a \iff a=dq
$$
for some $q\in \mathbb{Z}$, then $d$ is a divisor of $a$.

If $a\ne 0$ and $a=dq$, then $q\ne 0$, so $|q|\ge 1$, and

$$
|a|=|dq|=|d||q|\ge |d|
$$

Now, we define 

$$
D(a)\coloneqq \{n\in \mathbb{Z}\mid n \text{ divides } a \}
$$
is a finite set, as $D(a)\subseteq [-|a|,|a| ]$, and therefore:

$$
\operatorname{min}D(a)= -|a|,\qquad\operatorname{max}D(a)= |a|
$$



:::tip Greatest Common Divisor

Let $a,b$ be non-zero integers; the set of their common divisors is $D(a)\cap D(b)$, and their greatest common divisor is defined as:

$$
\operatorname{gcd}(a,b)\coloneqq \operatorname{max}\big(D(a)\cap D(b) \big)
$$

:::

## Counting Functions, Subsets, and Binomial Coefficients

### Counting Functions


Suppose $X$ and $Y$ are finite sets. To define a function $f:X\rightarrow Y$, we choose a value in $Y$ for every element in $X$.

::::info Example

If 

$$
X=\{a,b,c\},\qquad Y=\{d,e \} 
$$

then each $a,b,c$ has two choices; hence, there are $2\cdot 2\cdot 2=8$ total functions.


::::

:::info Proposition

Let $X$ and $Y$ be nonempty sets with $|X|=m$ and $|Y|=n$, then the number of functions $f:X\rightarrow Y$ is $n^m$

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

We write 

$$
X=\{x_1,\dots,x_m\},\qquad Y=\{y_1,\dots,y_n \}
$$
A function is determined by the list $f(x_1),\dots, f(x_m)$, and as each value has $n$ possible choices, independent of each other, the total number is $\underbrace{n\cdot n \cdots n}_{m \text{ factors}} =n^{m}$

</details>

:::

:::note Notation

For sets $X$ and $Y$, let $\operatorname{Fun}(X,Y)=\{f\mid f:X\rightarrow Y \}$

Thus for finite sets, $|\operatorname{Fun}(X,Y)|= |Y|^{|X|}$

:::

::::tip Injective Function

Let

$$
\operatorname{Inj}(X,Y)=\{f\mid f:X\rightarrow Y\text{ is injective} \}
$$

:::info Proposition

Let $|X|=m$ and $|Y|=n$, with $m\le n$, then

$$
|\operatorname{Inj}(X,Y)|=n(n-1)\cdots (n-m+1)=\frac{n!}{(n-m)!}
$$

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

Building an injection amounts to assigning, to each element of $X$ in turn, a distinct element of $Y$ not yet used:

The first element of $X$ may be sent to any of the $n$ elements of $Y$; since the map must stay injective, the second element has only $n-1$ remaining choices, the third has $n-2$, and so on, until the $m$-th element, which has $n-m+1$ choices left. Multiplying these counts over all $m$ elements gives:

$$
|\operatorname{Inj}(X,Y)|=n(n-1)\cdots (n-m+1)=\frac{n!}{(n-m)!}\tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$

</details>

:::

:::danger Remark

If $m>n$, then $|\operatorname{Inj}(X,Y)|=0$

:::

:::tip Permutation

A bijection $f:X\rightarrow X$ is called a **Permutation** of $X$

Moreover, if $X$ is a finite set with $|X|=n$, then the number of permutations of $X$ is $n!$

:::

### Counting Subsets

::::tip Characteristic Function

For each $A\subseteq X$, its **Characteristic Function**

$$
\chi_A:X\longrightarrow \{0,1\}
$$
is defined by 
$$
\chi_A(x)=\begin{cases}
1\quad &\text{if }x\in A\\
0\quad &\text{if }x\notin A
\end{cases}
$$

:::info Proposition 
If $X$ is a finite set, then the cardinality of the power set is

$$
|\mathcal{P}(X)|=2^{|X|}
$$

### Binomial Coefficient

::::tip Binomial Coefficient

Let $X$ be a set and $r$ be a non-negative integer, then an $r$-subset of $X$ is a subset $A\subseteq X$ with $|A|=r$, written as:

$$
\mathcal{P}_{r}(X)=\{A\subseteq X\mid |A|=r \}\subseteq \mathcal{P}(X)
$$

If $|X|=n$, then we define the **Binomial Coefficient**:

$$
\binom{n}{r}\coloneqq|\mathcal{P}_{r}(X)|
$$

:::info Propositions

1. If $r>n$, then 
$$
    \binom{n}{r}=0
$$
<hr/>

2. 
$$
    \binom{n}{0}=1,\quad \binom{n}{1}=n,\quad \binom{n}{n}=1
$$
<hr/>

3. For $0\le r\le n$:
$$
    \binom{n}{r}=\binom{n}{n-r}
$$
<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

</details>

:::

::::

:::tip Corollary

For every non-negative integer $n$:

$$
\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}=2^{n}
$$

</details>

:::tip Pascal's Identity

For positive integers $n,r$ with $1\le r\le n$,

$$
\binom{n}{r}=\binom{n-1}{r-1}+\binom{n-1}{r}
$$

<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

For $0\le r\le n$:

$$
\binom{n}{r}=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!r!}
$$
