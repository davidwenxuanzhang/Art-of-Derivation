## Direct Proof

### Divisibility 
::::tip Divisibility 

Given two integers $a$ and $b$, we say that $b$ divides $a$ or $a$ is a multiple of $b$ to mean that there is an **unique** integer $q$ such that $a=bq$

:::note Why Unique

The reason that we say it is **unique** integer is because for the proposition "$0 \text{ divides }0$", it is true for all integers $q$, but obviously $0$ cannot divides anything; therefore, **unique** is really important.
:::
Notation:

$$
b \mid a : b \text{ divides } a
$$

::::

### Even Number and Odd Number

:::tip Even Number
With the (definition), we define:

$$
a \text{ is even if } 2\mid a
$$
:::
:::tip Odd Number
Similarly, with the (definition), we define:

$$
a \text{ is odd (not even) if } 2\nmid a
$$
:::

::::info Example

Prove $101$ is an odd number.

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

:::danger Warning

For the purpose of this class, you cannot just say $101\div 2=50.5\implies \text{not even}$. This will recieve a 0, and you have to use what we learn and prove it.

:::

Let $q$ be an integer

- Case 1: $q < 50 \implies 2q<100$ by [Multiplication law](docs/Notes/Stony-Brook/MAT250/9-15-2026.mdx#Order-Axioms)
- Case 2: $q = 50 \implies 2q=100 $
- Case 4: $q > 50 \implies q \ge 51 \implies 2q \ge 102$

Therefore: $101$ is not even $\implies $ $101$ is odd 

<div style={{ textAlign: 'right' }}>
  $\blacksquare$
</div>
</details>

## Proof by Contradiction

:::::tip Proof by Contradiction

If we want to prove $P$ is true:

Start with assume $\neg P$ is true, then implies something always false, also known as [contradiction](docs/Notes/Stony-Brook/MAT200/8-25-2026.mdx#Contradiction) $(Q\land \neg Q)$

::::info Example

There do not exists integers $m,n$ such that $2m+4n=101$

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

Suppose there are such integers $m$ and $n$ so that 2m+4n=101

$$
\begin{align*}
    &\implies 2(m+2n)=101 &\qquad \text{(Distributivity)} \\
    &\implies 2\mid 101 
    &\implies 101 \text{ is even}
\end{align*}
$$
However, from [previous example](#even-number-and-odd-number), $101$ is odd, and this gives us a **contradiction**.

:::danger Warning
You must mention the Contradiction
:::

Thus, there is no such integers. 
<div style={{ textAlign: 'right' }}>
  $\blacksquare$
</div>
</details>
::::

<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

           &=2a\times b\\
           &=2ab &\qquad \text{(Given)}\\
           \implies a^2+b^2&\le 2ab &\qquad  \text{(Create Contradiction)}
\end{align*}
$$
As $a^2+b^2>2ab \iff \neg(a^2+b^2\le 2ab)$, this gives a contradiction.

Thus $a\ne b $

## Proof by Contrapositive


::::tip Proof by Contrapositive
Recall that the [contrapositive](docs\Notes\Stony-Brook\MAT200\8-27-2026.mdx#Contrapositive) statement of $P\implies Q$ is:

$$
\neg Q\implies \neg P  
$$
More importantly: 

$$
 P\implies Q\equiv \neg Q\implies \neg P
$$
Therefore, to prove the statement $P\implies Q$, we can alternatively prove $\neg Q\implies \neg P$

:::info Example

Prove that if $a\le b$ and $a\ge b$, then $a=b$

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

Let $P: a\le b \land a\ge b$ and $Q:a=b$

Then, $\neg Q\equiv a\ne b$ and $\neg P \equiv a<b \lor  a>b$

Assume $\neg P$ which $a\ne b$, then by [Law of Trichotomy](docs\Notes\Stony-Brook\MAT250\9-15-2026.mdx#Order-Axioms), either $a>b$ nor $a<b$, which is $\neg Q$.

Therefore, by contrapositive:

$$
a\le b \land a\ge b \implies a=b \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$


</details>

:::


::::


## Proving by "Or" Statement

::::tip Proving by "Or" Statement

Recall that:

$$
P\lor Q \equiv \neg P\implies Q
$$

This useful equivalence might be handy when proving conclusion including "or".

:::info Example

Let $a,b \in \mathbb{R}$, then $ab=0$ if and only if $a=0$ or $b=0$

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

1. $\Longleftarrow $ Direction: If $a=0 \lor b=0$, then:

$$
ab=0\times b = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad ab=a\times 0 = 0
$$
Thus:

$$
a=0 \lor b=0 \implies ab=0
$$

<hr/>

2. $\implies$ Direction: Suppose $ab=0$, then our goal is prving $a=0 \lor b=0$

Using the equivalence $P\lor Q \equiv (\neg P \implies Q)$, assume $a\ne 0$, then $ax=1$ has a unique solution $\frac{1}{a}$ according to [unity](docs\Notes\Stony-Brook\MAT250\9-15-2026.mdx#Sum-and-Product), and from given:

$$
\begin{align*}
    ab&=0\\
    \frac{1}{a}\times (ab)&= \frac{1}{a}\times 0\\
    (\frac{1}{a}\times a)\times b&= 0\\
    1\times b&=0\\
    b&=0
\end{align*}
$$

Thus: 

$$
ab=0 \implies (a\ne 0 \implies b=0) \equiv ab=0\implies a=0 \lor b=0
$$

<hr/>

Therefore: 

$$
ab=0 \iff a=0 \lor b=0 \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$

</details>

:::

## Mathematical Induction

::::tip Induction Principle

Let $P(n)$ be a statement involving a general positive integers $n$, then $P(n)$ is true for every positive $n$ provided that:

1.  $P(n)$ is true

2. For every positive integer $k$, the implication: 

   $$
   P(k) \implies P(k+1)
   $$ 

   is true.

:::danger Natural Numbers Only

Mathematical Induction involve natural numbers only.
