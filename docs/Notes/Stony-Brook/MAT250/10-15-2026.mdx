## Functions

### Sequences

:::tip Sequences

A function $f:\mathbb{Z}^{+}\rightarrow A$ is called a sequence in the set $A$.

:::

### Intro about Image

:::tip Intro about Image

Let $f:X\rightarrow Y$. The **image** of $f$ is the subset of the codomain consisting of all values of $f$: 

$$
\operatorname{Im}(f)=\{f(x)\mid x \in X \}\subseteq Y
$$

:::

### Graph

::::tip Graph

The **graph** of a function $f:X\rightarrow Y$ is the subset of $X\times Y$ given by:

$$
G_{f}=\{(x,y)\in X\times Y \mid y=f(x)\}=\{\big(x,f(x)\big) \mid x\in X\}
$$

:::danger Remark

The graph of $f:X\rightarrow Y$ is a particular subset of $X\times Y$, determined by the predicate $y=f(x)$. Not every subset of $X\times Y$ is the graph of a function.

:::
::::

### Composition

::::tip Composition

Given functions:

$$
f:X\longrightarrow Y,\qquad g:Y\longrightarrow Z
$$

the **composition** of $f$ and $g$ is: 

$$
g\circ f:X\longrightarrow Z
$$

defined by 
$$
(g\circ f)(x)=g\big(f(x)\big)
$$

:::info Example

Let $f,g: \mathbb{R}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be given by:

$$
f(x)=x+1,\qquad g(x)=x^2
$$

Then, 

$$
\begin{align*}
    (f\circ g)(x)&=f\big(g(x)\big)=f(x^2)=x^2+1\\
    (g\circ f)(x)&=g\big(f(x)\big)=g(x+1)=(x+1)^2
\end{align*}
$$

As $f\circ g \ne g\circ f$, the composition is not commutative in general.

:::

:::info Associative

Composition is associative. If:

$$
f:X\rightarrow Y,\qquad g:Y\rightarrow Z,\qquad h:Z\rightarrow W
$$
then
$$
(h\circ g)\circ f=h\circ (g\circ f):X\rightarrow W
$$

:::


:::danger Remark

If $f:X\rightarrow Y$, then identity functions satisfy:

$$
f\circ \operatorname{id}_{X}=f=\operatorname{id}_Y\circ f
$$

:::


::::

### Images and Preimages of Sets

#### Image

:::tip Image

Let $f:X\rightarrow Y$. For $A\subseteq X$, the image of $A$ under $f$ is:

$$
f(A)=\{f(x)\mid x\in A\}\subseteq Y
$$
Or in a more favorable form:

$$
y\in f(A)\iff \exists x\in A \text{ such that }f(x)=y
$$
:::

#### Preimage

:::tip Preimage

Let $f:X\rightarrow Y$. For $B\subseteq Y$, the preimage of $B$ under $f$ is:

$$
f^{-1}(B)=\{x\in X\mid f(x)\in B \}\subseteq X
$$
Or in a more favorable form:

$$
x\in f^{-1}(B)\iff f(x)\in B
$$
:::

:::danger Remark

$$
f(X)=\operatorname{Im}(f)\quad \text{and}\quad f(A)=\operatorname{Im}(f|_{A})
$$

:::

:::info Propositions

Let $A, B\subseteq X$ and $C,D\subseteq Y$, then:

1. 
    $$
    f(A\cup B)=f(A)\cup f(B)
    $$
<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

- $\subseteq$ direction: 

    Take $y\in f(A\cup B)$, according to the definition of [image](#image):

    $$
    \begin{align*}
        y\in f(A\cup B)
        &\iff \exists x\in A\cup B,\ y=f(x)\\
        &\iff \exists x\,[(x\in A\lor x\in B)\land y=f(x)]\\
        &\iff (\exists a\in A,\ y=f(a))
        \lor(\exists b\in B,\ y=f(b))\\
        &\iff (y\in f(A))\lor(y\in f(B))\\
        &\iff y\in f(A)\cup f(B)
    \end{align*}
    $$
    Therefore, as $y\in f(A\cup B)\implies y\in f(A)\cup f(B)$
    $$
    f(A\cup B)\subseteq f(A)\cup f(B)
    $$

- $\supseteq$ direction:

    Following the chain in the opposite direction, $y \in f(A)\cup f(B)\implies y\in f(A\cup B)$, so

    $$
    f(A)\cup f(B)\subseteq f(A\cup B)
    $$
Therefore:

$$
f(A\cup B)=f(A)\cup f(B) \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$
</details>
<hr/>

2. 
    $$
    f^{-1}(C\cup D)=f^{-1}(C)\cup f^{-1}(D)
    $$
<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

- $\subseteq$ direction: 

    Take $x\in f^{-1}(C\cup D)$, according to the definition of [preimage](#preimage):

    $$
    \begin{align*}
        x\in f^{-1}(C\cup D)
        &\iff  f(x)\in C\cup D\\
        &\iff f(x)\in C \lor f(x)\in D\\
        &\iff x\in f^{-1}(C)\lor x\in f^{-1}(D)\\
        &\iff x\in f^{-1}(C)\cup f^{-1}(D)
    \end{align*}
    $$
    Therefore, as $x\in f^{-1}(C\cup D)\implies x\in f^{-1}(C)\cup f^{-1}(D)$
    $$
    f^{-1}(C\cup D)\subseteq f^{-1}(C)\cup f^{-1}(D)
    $$

- $\supseteq$ direction:

    Following the chain in the opposite direction, $x \in f^{-1}(C)\cup f^{-1}(D)\implies x\in f^{-1}(C\cup D)$, so

    $$
    f^{-1}(C)\cup f^{-1}(D)\subseteq f^{-1}(C\cup D)
    $$
Therefore:

$$
f^{-1}(C\cup D)= f^{-1}(C)\cup f^{-1}(D) \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$

</details>
<hr/>

3.  
    $$
    f(A\cap B)\subseteq f(A)\cap f(B)
    $$
<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

Take $y\in f(A\cap B)$, according to the definition of [image](#image):

$$
\begin{align*}
        y\in f(A\cap B)
        &\iff \exists x\in A\cap B,\ y=f(x)\\
        &\iff \exists x\,[(x\in A\land x\in B)\land y=f(x)]\\
        &\implies (\exists a\in A,\ y=f(a))
        \land (\exists b\in B,\ y=f(b))\\
        &\iff (y\in f(A))\land (y\in f(B))\\
        &\iff y\in f(A)\cap f(B)
\end{align*}
$$
Therefore, as $y\in f(A\cap B)\implies y\in f(A)\cap f(B)$
$$
    f(A\cap B)\subseteq f(A)\cap f(B) \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$

</details>
<hr/>

4. 
    $$
    f^{-1}(C\cap D)= f^{-1}(C)\cap f^{-1}(D)
    $$
<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

- $\subseteq$ direction: 

    Take $x\in f^{-1}(C\cap D)$, according to the definition of [preimage](#preimage):

    $$
    \begin{align*}
        x\in f^{-1}(C\cap D)
        &\iff  f(x)\in C\cap D\\
        &\iff f(x)\in C \land f(x)\in D\\
        &\iff x\in f^{-1}(C)\land x\in f^{-1}(D)\\
        &\iff x\in f^{-1}(C)\cap f^{-1}(D)
    \end{align*}
    $$
    Therefore, as $x\in f^{-1}(C\cap D)\implies x\in f^{-1}(C)\cap f^{-1}(D)$
    $$
    f^{-1}(C\cap D)\subseteq f^{-1}(C)\cap f^{-1}(D)
    $$

- $\supseteq$ direction:

    Following the chain in the opposite direction, $x \in f^{-1}(C)\cap f^{-1}(D)\implies x\in f^{-1}(C\cap D)$, so

    $$
    f^{-1}(C)\cap f^{-1}(D)\subseteq f^{-1}(C\cap D)
    $$
Therefore:

$$
f^{-1}(C\cap D)= f^{-1}(C)\cap f^{-1}(D) \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$

</details>
:::

## Injective, Surjective, and Bijective Functions

### Injective Functions

::::tip Injective Functions
A function $f:X\rightarrow Y$ is **injective**, or **one-to-one**, if every $y\in Y$ has at most one preimage under $f$. Equivalently,
$$
x_1\ne x_2\implies f(x_1)\ne f(x_2)
$$
for all $x_1,x_2\in X$, or, contrapositively

$$
f(x_1)= f(x_2) \implies x_1= x_2
$$

:::danger Remark
The function $f:X\rightarrow Y$ is injective if and only if

$$
f^{-1}(\{y\})
$$
is a singleton for every $y\in \operatorname{Im}(f)$
:::

:::info Example
The function $f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ defined by
$$
f(x)=2x+1
$$
is injective.

Indeed, if $f(x_1)=f(x_2)$, then

$$
2x_1+1=2x_2+1\implies x_1=x_2
$$
:::

:::info Non-Example
The function $f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ defined by

$$
f(x)=x^2
$$
is not injective because
$$
f(-1)=f(1)=1\text{ while }1\ne -1
$$

:::

::::

### Surjective Functions

::::tip Surjective Functions
A function $f:X\rightarrow Y$ is **surjective**, or **onto**, if every $y\in Y$ has at least one preimage under $f$. Equivalently,
$$
\forall y\in Y,\ \exists x\in X\text{ such that }f(x)=y
$$

:::danger Remark

The function $f:X\rightarrow Y$ is surjective if and only if

$$
f^{-1}(\{y\})\ne \emptyset
$$
for every $y\in Y$
:::

:::info Example
The function $f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ defined by
$$
f(x)=3x+1
$$
is surjective.

Indeed, given $y\in \mathbb{R}$, choose

$$
x=\frac{y-1}{3}
$$
then 

$$
f(x)=3\left(\frac{y-1}{3}\right)+1=y
$$
:::

:::info Non-Example
The function $f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ defined by
$$
f(x)=x^2
$$
is not surjective. For example, $-1$ has no preimage, since the equation $x^2=-1$ has no real solution.

:::

::::

### Bijective Function

:::tip Bijective Function

A function $f:X\rightarrow Y$ is **bijective** if it is both injective and surjective. Equivalently:

$$
\forall y\in Y,\ \exists !x\in X\text{ such that }f(x)=y
$$
Thus, a bijection gives a one-to-one correspondence between $X$ and $Y$.
:::

### Invertibility

:::tip Invertibility

A function $f:X\rightarrow Y$ is **invertible** if there exists a function $g:Y\rightarrow X$ such that

$$
g\circ f=\operatorname{id}_X\qquad \text{and}\qquad f\circ g=\operatorname{id}_Y
$$

:::
