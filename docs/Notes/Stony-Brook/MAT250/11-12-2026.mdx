## Division Theorem

::::tip Division Theorem

Given $a,b\in \mathbb{Z}$; if $b>0$, then exists unique $q$ and $r$ such that:

$$
a=bq+r, \qquad 0\le r <b
$$

:::danger Quotient and Remainder

The integer $q$ is called the **Quotient**, and the integer $r$ is called the **Remainder**

:::

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

We will do this <span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span> step by step: we prove the existence of $q$ and $r$ first, and then prove the uniqueness of them.

- Existence of $q$ and $r$

    There are two cases:
    - Case 1: $a\ge 0$
        
        Consider the set:
        $$
        A=\{k\in \mathbb{Z}:k\ge 0 \text{ and }bk\le a \}
        $$

        First, $A$ is non-empty, as 
        $$
        a \in A\qquad b\cdot 0=0\subseteq a
        $$

        If $m$ is anything from $a$ ($m\in A$), then
        $$
        0\le m \text{ and }bm\le a\underbrace{\implies}_{b\ge 1} m\le mb\le a\implies m\le a
        $$
        Thus, $A$ is a finite set, and therefore a maximum element: $\operatorname{max}A=q$ exists. Moreover, let

        $$
        r=a-bq
        $$

        Since $q\in A$, we have:

        $$
        bq\le a \implies r=a-bq \ge 0
        $$

        Now, set up a contradiction (claude: add a link to contradiction): if $r\ge b$, then

        $$
        r=a-bq\ge b \ge b \implies a\ge b+bq= (1+q)b\implies q+1\in A
        $$

        This contradict with the fact that $q=\operatorname{max}A$, so $r\ge b$ is false, and thus $r<b$. Consequently:

        $$
        a=bq+r,\qquad 0\le r<b
        $$
    - Case 2: $a<0$

        When $a<0$, then $-a>0$, and by case 1 we know there exist integer $q_1$ and $r_1$ such that:

        $$
        -a=bq_1+r_1, \qquad 0\le r_1 <b
        $$
        $$
        \begin{align*}
            \implies a &= b(-q_1)-r_1\\
            &=b(-q_1-1)+b-r_1\\
            &=b(-q_1-1)+(b-r_1)
        \end{align*}
        $$
        $$
        q=-q_1=1,\qquad r=b-r_1
        $$
        $$
        \begin{align*}
            -b&<-r_1\le 0\\
            -b+b&<-r_1+b \le b \\
            0&<-r_1+b\le b\\
        \end{align*}
        $$

        - Subcase $r_1=0$:
            $$
            \begin{align*}
                -a&=bq_1\\
                a&=b(-q_1)\\
                \text{let }q&=-q_1 \text{ and }r=0\\
                0&\le r < b
            \end{align*}
            $$
        - Subcase $r_1>0$
            $$
            \begin{align*}
                0&<r_1<b\\
                -b&<-r_1<0\\
                0&<b-r_1<b
            \end{align*}
            $$
    Thus, in both cases, there exist integer $q$ and $r$ satisfying

    $$
    a=bq+r,\qquad 0\le r<b
    $$

    So the existence proved.

<hr/>

- Uniqueness of $q$ and $r$

    Assume there are integers $q_1$, $r_1$, $q_2$, $r_1$ such that 

    $$
    \begin{align*}
    a+bq_1+r_1,\qquad 0\le r_1<b\\
    a+bq_2+r_2,\qquad 0\le r_2<b
    \end{align*}
    $$
    $$
    \begin{align*}
    \text{assume }q_1\ge  q_2&\implies bq_1\ge bq_2\\
    &\implies -bq_1\le -bq_2\\
    &\implies a-bq_1\le a-bq_2\\
    &\implies r_q\le r_2\\
    &\implies 0\le r_2-r_1<b\\
    &\implies 0\le (a-bq_1)-(a-bq_2)<b\\
    &\implies 0\le -bq_1+bq_2<b\\
    &\implies 0\le b(q_2-q_1)<b\\
    &\implies 0\le q_2-q_1<1\\
    &\implies q_2-q_1=0\\
    &\implies q_2=q_1
    \end{align*}
    $$
    Without lost of generality,
    $$
    r_1=a-bq_1=a-bq_2=r_2
    $$

Therefore, both the quotient and the remainder are unique, and with the existence of quotient and the remainder, the prove for division theorem is done. (claude: add a black square)

</details>

## Euclidean Algorithm


### Greatest Common Divisor

::::tip Greatest Common Divisor

Let $a$ and $b$ be two integers, at elast one of which is non-zero. The **Greatest Common Divisor** of $a$ and $b$ is the unique positive integer $d$ such that:
1. $d$ is a common divisor of $a$ and $b$:

$$
d\mid a,\qquad d\mid b
$$

2. If $c\mid a$ and $c\mid d$, then $c\le d$


:::note Notation

The Greatest Common Divisor of $a$ and $b$ is denoted by:
$$
(a,b)=\operatorname{gcd}(a,b)
$$
:::
:::info Lemma

For non-zero integer $a$ and $b$, if $b>0$ and $a=bq+r$ where $q,r\in \mathbb{Z}$, then:

$$
\operatorname{gcd}(a,b)=\operatorname{gcd}(b,r)
$$

:::

::::

### The Euclidean Algorithm

:::tip The Euclidean Algorithm

Suppose $a$ annd $b$ are positive integers; set $a_0=a$ and $a_1=b$

We define the Euclidean Algorithm recursive ly as below:

- Step $k$: for $k\ge 1$, suppose that $a_0,\dots, a_{k-1},a_k$ has been defined, then using the [devision theorem](#division-theorem):

$$
a_k-1=a_kq_k+r_k,\qquad 0\le r_k< a_k
$$

    If $r_k=0$, then stop.

    Otherwise, put $a_{k+1}=r_k$ and continue with the step $k+1$.

:::

## Consequences of the Euclidean Algorithm

$$
\operatorname{gcd}(a,b)=am+bn
