## Review Midterm 1

This review was generated by [ChatGPT](https://chatgpt.com/) and modified by [Claude Code](https:claude.com/), drawing on both the practice midterm and the real midterm exam. I can't guarantee it covers everything, but it should be enough to prepare.

This review summarizes the main ideas needed for Midterm 1: propositional logic, implication and logical equivalence, predicates and quantifiers, translating mathematical statements, negating quantified statements, quantifier order, unique existence, and logical regions in the coordinate plane.

:::info Central Strategy

For a complicated logical statement, work through these steps in order: **Scope**, then **Logical Structure**, then **Translation**, then **Simplification**, then **Truth/Proof**.

:::

### Propositions and Predicates

:::tip Definition

A **proposition** is a statement with a definite truth value.

A **predicate** contains variables and does not have a truth value until its variables are assigned or quantified.

:::

:::info Example

1. $P(x):x^2>4$  
    → a **Predicate**
2. $P(3)$  
    → a **Proposition**, because the variable has been assigned
3. $\forall x\in\mathbb{R},\ P(x)$  
    → a **Proposition**, because the variable has been quantified

:::

### Logical Connectives

<div style={{ width: 'fit-content', margin: '0 auto' }}>

| Name | Notation | Meaning |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Negation | $\neg P$ | True if $P$ is false, and false if $P$ is true |
| Conjunction | $P\land Q$ | True if both $P$ and $Q$ are true, and false otherwise |
| Disjunction | $P\lor Q$ | True if at least one of $P,Q$ is true, and false otherwise |
| Implication | $P\implies Q$ | False only when $P$ is true and $Q$ is false |
| Biconditional | $P\iff Q$ | True when $P$ and $Q$ have the same truth value |

</div>

:::note Inclusive OR

In mathematical logic, OR is normally **inclusive**: if both $P$ and $Q$ are true, $P\lor Q$ is still true.

:::

### Implication

The statement $P\implies Q$ means "if $P$ is true, then $Q$ must be true."

<div style={{ width: 'fit-content', margin: '0 auto' }}>

| $P$ | $Q$ | $P\implies Q$ |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| T | T | T |
| T | F | F |
| F | T | T |
| F | F | T |

</div>

:::danger Key Fact

An implication is false in exactly one situation: when $P$ is true and $Q$ is false.

:::

#### Implication as OR

One of the most important equivalences is $P\implies Q\equiv \neg P\lor Q$.

<div style={{ width: 'fit-content', margin: '0 auto' }}>

| $P$ | $Q$ | $P\implies Q$ | $\neg P$ | $\neg P\lor Q$ |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| T | T | <span style={{color:'#2563eb'}}>T</span> | F | <span style={{color:'#2563eb'}}>T</span> |
| T | F | <span style={{color:'#2563eb'}}>F</span> | F | <span style={{color:'#2563eb'}}>F</span> |
| F | T | <span style={{color:'#2563eb'}}>T</span> | T | <span style={{color:'#2563eb'}}>T</span> |
| F | F | <span style={{color:'#2563eb'}}>T</span> | T | <span style={{color:'#2563eb'}}>T</span> |

</div>

This identity is extremely useful for simplifying logical expressions.

#### Negating an Implication

$$
\neg(P\implies Q)\equiv P\land\neg Q
$$

:::danger Common Mistake

The negation of an implication is **not** the inverse $\neg P\implies\neg Q$.

:::

### De Morgan's Laws

You should know both forms immediately:

$$
\neg(P\land Q)\equiv \neg P\lor\neg Q \qquad\text{and}\qquad \neg(P\lor Q)\equiv \neg P\land\neg Q
$$

<div style={{ width: 'fit-content', margin: '0 auto', overflowX: 'auto' }}>

| $P$ | $Q$ | $P\land Q$ | $\neg(P\land Q)$ | $P\lor Q$ | $\neg(P\lor Q)$ | $\neg P$ | $\neg Q$ | $\neg P\lor\neg Q$ | $\neg P\land\neg Q$ |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| T | T | T | <span style={{color:'#2563eb'}}>F</span> | T | <span style={{color:'#ea580c'}}>F</span> | F | F | <span style={{color:'#2563eb'}}>F</span> | <span style={{color:'#ea580c'}}>F</span> |
| T | F | F | <span style={{color:'#2563eb'}}>T</span> | T | <span style={{color:'#ea580c'}}>F</span> | F | T | <span style={{color:'#2563eb'}}>T</span> | <span style={{color:'#ea580c'}}>F</span> |
| F | T | F | <span style={{color:'#2563eb'}}>T</span> | T | <span style={{color:'#ea580c'}}>F</span> | T | F | <span style={{color:'#2563eb'}}>T</span> | <span style={{color:'#ea580c'}}>F</span> |
| F | F | F | <span style={{color:'#2563eb'}}>T</span> | F | <span style={{color:'#ea580c'}}>T</span> | T | T | <span style={{color:'#2563eb'}}>T</span> | <span style={{color:'#ea580c'}}>T</span> |

</div>

A useful way to remember this: negation swaps $\land$ and $\lor$.

### Biconditional

The statement $P\iff Q$ means $P$ and $Q$ have the same truth value. It expands as:

$$
P\iff Q \equiv (P\implies Q)\land(Q\implies P)
$$

Therefore an "if and only if" statement requires both directions.

#### Negating a Biconditional

The negation is true exactly when $P$ and $Q$ have different truth values:

$$
\neg(P\iff Q)\equiv (P\land\neg Q)\lor(\neg P\land Q)
$$

:::info Interpretation

A negated biconditional means exactly one of the two statements is true.

:::

### Logical Equivalence

:::tip Definition

Two statements are logically equivalent, $A\equiv B$, if they have the same truth value for every possible assignment.

:::

There are two main ways to prove equivalence: construct a complete truth table, or transform one expression into the other using logical laws.

:::warning Example

Simplify $(P\land\neg Q)\implies R$

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

$$
\begin{align*}
(P\land\neg Q)\implies R&\equiv \neg(P\land\neg Q)\lor R &\qquad \text{(Implication as OR)}\\
                    &\equiv (\neg P\lor Q)\lor R &\qquad \text{(De Morgan's Law)}\\
                    &\equiv \neg P\lor Q\lor R \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
\end{align*}
$$

</details>

:::

### Converse, Inverse, and Contrapositive

Starting from $P\implies Q$:

- Converse: $Q\implies P$
- Inverse: $\neg P\implies\neg Q$
- Contrapositive: $\neg Q\implies\neg P$

The important equivalences are:

$$
P\implies Q\equiv \neg Q\implies\neg P \qquad\text{and}\qquad Q\implies P\equiv \neg P\implies\neg Q
$$

:::danger Remark

The original statement is generally **not** equivalent to its converse.

:::

### Necessary and Sufficient Conditions

Suppose $P\implies Q$. Then $P$ is **sufficient** for $Q$, and $Q$ is **necessary** for $P$. That is, "$P$ is sufficient for $Q$" means $P\implies Q$, while "$P$ is necessary for $Q$" means $Q\implies P$.

#### Translation Patterns

Memorize these patterns.

<div style={{ width: 'fit-content', margin: '0 auto' }}>

| Phrase | Symbolic Form |
|:---|:---:|
| If $P$, then $Q$ | $P\implies Q$ |
| $P$ if $Q$ | $Q\implies P$ |
| $P$ only if $Q$ | $P\implies Q$ |
| $P$ is sufficient for $Q$ | $P\implies Q$ |
| $P$ is necessary for $Q$ | $Q\implies P$ |
| $P$ if and only if $Q$ | $P\iff Q$ |

</div>

:::danger Translation Trap

The word **necessary** often reverses the order people expect: if $A$ is necessary for $B$, then $B\implies A$.

:::

### Operator Precedence

When parentheses are omitted, a useful precedence convention is

$$
\neg>\land>\lor>\implies>\iff
$$

For example, $P\land Q\lor R$ means $(P\land Q)\lor R$, and $P\lor Q\implies R$ means $(P\lor Q)\implies R$.

:::danger Advice

For complicated expressions, add parentheses yourself — do not rely on precedence when you can make the structure explicit.

:::

### Tautology, Contradiction, and Contingency

:::tip Definitions

A **tautology** is always true. A **contradiction** is always false. A **contingency** is true under some assignments and false under others.

:::

:::info Example

1. $P\lor\neg P$ is a **Tautology**
2. $P\land\neg P$ is a **Contradiction**

:::

### Universal and Existential Quantifiers

The **universal quantifier** $\forall$ means "for every," and the **existential quantifier** $\exists$ means "there exists at least one."

:::info Example

1. $\forall x\in\mathbb{R},\ x^2\ge 0$  
    → a true quantified proposition
2. $\exists x\in\mathbb{R},\ x^2=4$  
    → a true quantified proposition

:::

:::danger Reminder

$\exists$ means **at least one** — it does not mean unique. Exactly one requires $\exists!$.

:::

### Free and Bound Variables

A variable controlled by a quantifier is **bound**. A variable not controlled by a quantifier is **free**.

:::info Example

1. $\forall x\,P(x,y)$ → $x$ is bound, $y$ is free
2. $\exists x\big(P(x)\land \forall y\,Q(x,y)\big)$ → both $x$ and $y$ are bound

:::

:::note Scope

The outer quantifier controls every occurrence of $x$ within its scope, so the same chosen $x$ appears in both predicates.

:::

### Quantifier Order

This distinction is extremely important. $\forall x\,\exists y\,P(x,y)$ means "for every $x$, we can find a $y$" — the choice of $y$ may depend on $x$. But $\exists y\,\forall x\,P(x,y)$ means "there is one fixed $y$ that works for every $x$." These statements are generally different.

:::info Example

Let $P(x,y):x+y=0$ over $\mathbb{R}$.

- $\forall x\,\exists y\,P(x,y)$ is **true** — for each $x$, choose $y=-x$.
- $\exists y\,\forall x\,P(x,y)$ is **false** — no single $y$ works for every real $x$.

:::

### When Quantifiers Commute

Quantifiers of the same type can switch order:

$$
\forall x\forall y\,P(x,y)\equiv \forall y\forall x\,P(x,y) \qquad\text{and}\qquad \exists x\exists y\,P(x,y)\equiv \exists y\exists x\,P(x,y)
$$

However, $\forall x\exists y\,P(x,y)$ is generally **not** equivalent to $\exists y\forall x\,P(x,y)$. In short: $\forall\forall$ and $\exists\exists$ commute, but mixed quantifiers generally do not.

### Negating Quantified Statements

To negate a quantified statement, switch the quantifier and negate the predicate:

$$
\begin{align*}
\neg\big(\forall x\,P(x)\big)&\iff \exists x\,\neg P(x)\\
\neg\big(\exists x\,P(x)\big)&\iff \forall x\,\neg P(x)
\end{align*}
$$

For example, $\neg\forall x\exists y\forall z\,P(x,y,z)$ becomes $\exists x\forall y\exists z\,\neg P(x,y,z)$ — every quantifier changes type.

### Useful Denials

A useful denial pushes the negation inward until it reaches the basic predicates. For example, negating $\forall x\exists y\big(P(x,y)\implies Q(x,y)\big)$:

$$
\begin{align*}
\neg\Big(\forall x\exists y\big(P(x,y)\implies Q(x,y)\big)\Big)&\equiv \exists x\forall y\,\neg\big(P(x,y)\implies Q(x,y)\big) &\qquad \text{(switch quantifiers)}\\
&\equiv \exists x\forall y\big(P(x,y)\land \neg Q(x,y)\big) \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
\end{align*}
$$

This is much more useful than leaving the implication negated.

#### Negating Inequalities

<div style={{ width: 'fit-content', margin: '0 auto' }}>

| Statement | Negation |
|:---:|:---:|
| $x>a$ | $x\le a$ |
| $x\ge a$ | $x<a$ |
| $x<a$ | $x\ge a$ |
| $x\le a$ | $x>a$ |

</div>

:::danger Boundary Warning

The negation of $x>1$ is $x\le 1$, **not** $x<1$.

:::

### How to Prove Quantified Statements

Different quantifier structures require different strategies.

<div style={{ width: 'fit-content', margin: '0 auto' }}>

| Statement | Strategy |
|:---|:---|
| $\forall x\,P(x)$ | Start with an arbitrary $x$ and prove the property; to disprove, find one counterexample |
| $\exists x\,P(x)$ | Give one witness |
| $\forall x\,\exists y\,P(x,y)$ | Start with an arbitrary $x$ and construct a suitable $y$ (may depend on $x$) |
| $\exists x\,\forall y\,P(x,y)$ | Find one fixed $x$ and prove it works for every $y$ |

</div>

### Two-Variable Predicates

For a predicate $P(x,y)$, the four canonical quantifier patterns are:

$$
\forall x\forall y\,P(x,y) \qquad \forall x\exists y\,P(x,y) \qquad \exists x\forall y\,P(x,y) \qquad \exists x\exists y\,P(x,y)
$$

Since $\forall\forall$ and $\exists\exists$ commute, swapping $x$ and $y$ in the first and last forms gives an equivalent statement. The two mixed forms are the ones that need separate analysis.

### Unique Existence

$\exists! x\,P(x)$ means exactly one $x$ satisfies $P$, which requires both existence and uniqueness:

$$
\exists! x\,P(x) \equiv \exists x\Big(P(x)\land \forall y\big(P(y)\implies y=x\big)\Big)
$$

The first part says a solution exists; the second part says every solution must equal the chosen one.

### Negating Unique Existence

The negation of "exactly one solution" means either there are no solutions, or there are at least two different solutions:

$$
\neg\exists! x\,P(x) \equiv \Big(\forall x\,\neg P(x)\Big)\lor \Big(\exists x\exists y\big(P(x)\land P(y)\land x\ne y\big)\Big)
$$

:::info Best Way to Remember

**Not exactly one** = **zero** or **at least two**.

:::

### Logic and Coordinate Regions

Logical statements can describe regions in the plane. Suppose $P(x,y):x+y>1$ and $Q(x,y):xy<0$. Then $P\land Q$ is the intersection of the two regions, $P\lor Q$ is their union, and $\neg P$ is the complement of the $P$ region:

<div style={{ width: 'fit-content', margin: '0 auto' }}>

| Connective | Region Operation |
|:---:|:---:|
| $\land$ | intersection |
| $\lor$ | union |
| $\neg$ | complement |

</div>

Always check whether each boundary is included. For example, $\neg(x+y>1)$ becomes $x+y\le 1$, so the boundary line is included.

#### Negated Biconditional

Since $\neg(P\iff Q)\equiv (P\land\neg Q)\lor(\neg P\land Q)$, the required region is where exactly one of $P$ and $Q$ is true.

### High-Risk Mistakes

:::danger Necessary vs. Sufficient

If $A$ is necessary for $B$: $B\implies A$.

:::

:::danger Negating an Implication

$\neg(P\implies Q)\equiv P\land\neg Q$

:::

:::danger Swapping Mixed Quantifiers

Generally, $\forall x\exists y$ cannot be changed to $\exists y\forall x$.

:::

:::danger Losing Quantifier Scope

Read the expression from the outside inward before manipulating it.

:::

:::danger Forgetting Equality

Negating a strict inequality introduces equality.

:::

:::danger Confusing Existence with Unique Existence

$\exists$ means at least one. $\exists!$ means exactly one.

:::

#### Question 1

:::warning Truth Table

Determine whether $\neg(P\implies Q)$ and $P\land\neg Q$ are logically equivalent.

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

<div style={{ width: 'fit-content', margin: '0 auto' }}>

| $P$ | $Q$ | $P\implies Q$ | $\neg(P\implies Q)$ | $\neg Q$ | $P\land\neg Q$ |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| T | T | T | <span style={{color:'#2563eb'}}>F</span> | F | <span style={{color:'#2563eb'}}>F</span> |

\neg(P\implies Q)\equiv P\land\neg Q \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$

#### Question 2

:::warning Translation and Denial

Translate: "For every real $x$, there exists a real $y$ such that $y>x$ is necessary for $y^2>x^2$." Then construct a useful denial.

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

Since $y>x$ is necessary for $y^2>x^2$, the implication points from the second condition to the first:

$$
\begin{align*}
&\forall x\in \mathbb{R}\,\exists y\in \mathbb{R}\,\big(y^2>x^2\implies y>x\big)
\end{align*}
$$

Negating gives:

$$
\begin{align*}
\exists x\in \mathbb{R}\,\forall y\in \mathbb{R}\,\neg\big(y^2>x^2\implies y>x\big)&\equiv \exists x\in \mathbb{R}\,\forall y\in \mathbb{R}\,\big(y^2>x^2\land y\le x\big) \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
\end{align*}
$$

</details>

:::

#### Question 3

:::warning Quantifier Order

Let $P(x,y): y>x$ over $\mathbb{R}$. Determine the truth values of $\forall x\exists y\,P(x,y)$ and $\exists y\forall x\,P(x,y)$.

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

For the first statement, given any $x$, choose $y=x+1$; hence $\forall x\exists y\,P(x,y)$ is **true**.

For the second statement, one fixed $y$ would have to be greater than every real number, and no such number exists; hence $\exists y\forall x\,P(x,y)$ is **false**. $\blacksquare$

</details>

:::

#### Question 4

:::warning Useful Denial

Negate $\forall x\exists y\big(P(x,y)\implies Q(y)\big)$.

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

$$
\begin{align*}
\neg\Big(\forall x\exists y\big(P(x,y)\implies Q(y)\big)\Big)&\equiv \exists x\forall y\,\neg\big(P(x,y)\implies Q(y)\big) &\qquad \text{(switch quantifiers)}\\
&\equiv \exists x\forall y\big(P(x,y)\land\neg Q(y)\big) \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
\end{align*}
$$

</details>

:::

#### Question 5

:::warning Unique Existence

Rewrite $\exists! x\,P(x)$ without using the unique-existence symbol.

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

A solution must exist, $P(x)$, and every other solution must equal it, $\forall y\big(P(y)\implies y=x\big)$. Therefore:

$$
\exists! x\,P(x) \equiv \exists x\Big(P(x)\land \forall y\big(P(y)\implies y=x\big)\Big) \tag*{$\blacksquare$}
$$

</details>

:::

### Final Checklist

:::info Before the Exam

Make sure you can do all of the following:

- construct a truth table
- identify when an implication is false
- eliminate or negate an implication
- use De Morgan's laws
- expand and negate a biconditional
- identify converse, inverse, and contrapositive
- distinguish necessary from sufficient, and translate if/only if/necessary/sufficient
- identify operator precedence
- distinguish propositions from predicates
- identify free and bound variables and quantifier scope
- distinguish universal and existential quantifiers, and know when they commute
- find witnesses and counterexamples
- negate nested quantified statements and construct useful denials
- negate inequalities correctly
- analyze two-variable predicates
- expand and negate unique existence
- convert logic into coordinate-plane regions, handling boundaries correctly

:::

:::tip Key Formulas

<div style={{ width: 'fit-content', margin: '0 auto' }}>

| Rule | Equivalence |
|:---|:---:|
| Implication as OR | $P\implies Q\equiv \neg P\lor Q$ |
| Negated Implication | $\neg(P\implies Q)\equiv P\land\neg Q$ |
| Biconditional | $P\iff Q\equiv (P\implies Q)\land(Q\implies P)$ |
| Negated Universal | $\neg\forall x\,P(x)\equiv \exists x\,\neg P(x)$ |
| Negated Existential | $\neg\exists x\,P(x)\equiv \forall x\,\neg P(x)$ |

</div>

Note that in general, $\forall x\exists y\,P(x,y)\not\equiv \exists y\forall x\,P(x,y)$.

If the structure of a statement is clear, most Midterm 1 problems become much easier.
