Review Midterm 1​

This review summarizes the main ideas needed for Midterm 1: propositional logic, implication and logical equivalence, predicates and quantifiers, translating mathematical statements, negating quantified statements, quantifier order, unique existence, and logical regions in the coordinate plane.

Central Strategy

For a complicated logical statement, work through these steps in order: Scope, then Logical Structure, then Translation, then Simplification, then Truth/Proof.

Propositions and Predicates​

Definition

A proposition is a statement with a definite truth value.

A predicate contains variables and does not have a truth value until its variables are assigned or quantified.

Example
  1. P(x):x2>4P(x):x^2>4P(x):x2>4
    → a Predicate
  2. P(3)P(3)P(3)
    → a Proposition, because the variable has been assigned
  3. ∀x∈R, P(x)\forall x\in\mathbb{R},\ P(x)∀x∈R, P(x)
    → a Proposition, because the variable has been quantified

Inclusive OR

In mathematical logic, OR is normally inclusive: if both PPP and QQQ are true, P∨QP\lor QP∨Q is still true.

Negating an Implication​

¬(P  ⟹  Q)≡P∧¬Q\neg(P\implies Q)\equiv P\land\neg Q¬(P⟹Q)≡P∧¬Q

Biconditional​

The statement P  ⟺  QP\iff QP⟺Q means PPP and QQQ have the same truth value. It expands as:

P  ⟺  Q≡(P  ⟹  Q)∧(Q  ⟹  P)P\iff Q \equiv (P\implies Q)\land(Q\implies P)P⟺Q≡(P⟹Q)∧(Q⟹P)

Therefore an "if and only if" statement requires both directions.

Negating a Biconditional​

The negation is true exactly when PPP and QQQ have different truth values:

¬(P  ⟺  Q)≡(P∧¬Q)∨(¬P∧Q)\neg(P\iff Q)\equiv (P\land\neg Q)\lor(\neg P\land Q)¬(P⟺Q)≡(P∧¬Q)∨(¬P∧Q)
Interpretation

A negated biconditional means exactly one of the two statements is true.

Logical Equivalence​

Definition

Two statements are logically equivalent, A≡BA\equiv BA≡B, if they have the same truth value for every possible assignment.

There are two main ways to prove equivalence: construct a complete truth table, or transform one expression into the other using logical laws.

Example

Simplify (P∧¬Q)  ⟹  R(P\land\neg Q)\implies R(P∧¬Q)⟹R

Derivation
(P∧¬Q)  ⟹  R≡¬(P∧¬Q)∨R(Implication as OR)≡(¬P∨Q)∨R(De Morgan’s Law)≡¬P∨Q∨R\begin{align*} (P\land\neg Q)\implies R&\equiv \neg(P\land\neg Q)\lor R &\qquad \text{(Implication as OR)}\\ &\equiv (\neg P\lor Q)\lor R &\qquad \text{(De Morgan's Law)}\\ &\equiv \neg P\lor Q\lor R \tag*{$\blacksquare$} \end{align*}(P∧¬Q)⟹R​≡¬(P∧¬Q)∨R≡(¬P∨Q)∨R≡¬P∨Q∨R​(Implication as OR)(De Morgan’s Law)​■​

Converse, Inverse, and Contrapositive​

Starting from P  ⟹  QP\implies QP⟹Q:

  • Converse: Q  ⟹  PQ\implies PQ⟹P
  • Inverse: ¬P  ⟹  ¬Q\neg P\implies\neg Q¬P⟹¬Q
  • Contrapositive: ¬Q  ⟹  ¬P\neg Q\implies\neg P¬Q⟹¬P

The important equivalences are:

P  ⟹  Q≡¬Q  ⟹  ¬PandQ  ⟹  P≡¬P  ⟹  ¬QP\implies Q\equiv \neg Q\implies\neg P \qquad\text{and}\qquad Q\implies P\equiv \neg P\implies\neg QP⟹Q≡¬Q⟹¬PandQ⟹P≡¬P⟹¬Q
Remark

The original statement is generally not equivalent to its converse.

Necessary and Sufficient Conditions​

Suppose P  ⟹  QP\implies QP⟹Q. Then PPP is sufficient for QQQ, and QQQ is necessary for PPP. That is, "PPP is sufficient for QQQ" means P  ⟹  QP\implies QP⟹Q, while "PPP is necessary for QQQ" means Q  ⟹  PQ\implies PQ⟹P.

Translation Patterns​

Memorize these patterns.

PhraseSymbolic Form
If PPP, then QQQP  ⟹  QP\implies QP⟹Q
PPP if QQQQ  ⟹  PQ\implies PQ⟹P
PPP only if QQQP  ⟹  QP\implies QP⟹Q
PPP is sufficient for QQQP  ⟹  QP\implies QP⟹Q
PPP is necessary for QQQQ  ⟹  PQ\implies PQ⟹P
PPP if and only if QQQP  ⟺  QP\iff QP⟺Q
Translation Trap

The word necessary often reverses the order people expect: if AAA is necessary for BBB, then B  ⟹  AB\implies AB⟹A.

Operator Precedence​

When parentheses are omitted, a useful precedence convention is

¬>∧>∨>  ⟹  >  ⟺  \neg>\land>\lor>\implies>\iff¬>∧>∨>⟹>⟺

For example, P∧Q∨RP\land Q\lor RP∧Q∨R means (P∧Q)∨R(P\land Q)\lor R(P∧Q)∨R, and P∨Q  ⟹  RP\lor Q\implies RP∨Q⟹R means (P∨Q)  ⟹  R(P\lor Q)\implies R(P∨Q)⟹R.

Advice

For complicated expressions, add parentheses yourself — do not rely on precedence when you can make the structure explicit.

Tautology, Contradiction, and Contingency​

Definitions

A tautology is always true. A contradiction is always false. A contingency is true under some assignments and false under others.

Example
  1. P∨¬PP\lor\neg PP∨¬P is a Tautology
  2. P∧¬PP\land\neg PP∧¬P is a Contradiction

Universal and Existential Quantifiers​

The universal quantifier ∀\forall∀ means "for every," and the existential quantifier ∃\exists∃ means "there exists at least one."

Example
  1. ∀x∈R, x2≥0\forall x\in\mathbb{R},\ x^2\ge 0∀x∈R, x2≥0
    → a true quantified proposition
  2. ∃x∈R, x2=4\exists x\in\mathbb{R},\ x^2=4∃x∈R, x2=4
    → a true quantified proposition
Reminder

∃\exists∃ means at least one — it does not mean unique. Exactly one requires ∃!\exists!∃!.

Free and Bound Variables​

A variable controlled by a quantifier is bound. A variable not controlled by a quantifier is free.

Example
  1. ∀x P(x,y)\forall x\,P(x,y)∀xP(x,y) → xxx is bound, yyy is free
  2. ∃x(P(x)∧∀y Q(x,y))\exists x\big(P(x)\land \forall y\,Q(x,y)\big)∃x(P(x)∧∀yQ(x,y)) → both xxx and yyy are bound
Scope

The outer quantifier controls every occurrence of xxx within its scope, so the same chosen xxx appears in both predicates.

Quantifier Order​

This distinction is extremely important. ∀x ∃y P(x,y)\forall x\,\exists y\,P(x,y)∀x∃yP(x,y) means "for every xxx, we can find a yyy" — the choice of yyy may depend on xxx. But ∃y ∀x P(x,y)\exists y\,\forall x\,P(x,y)∃y∀xP(x,y) means "there is one fixed yyy that works for every xxx." These statements are generally different.

Example

Let P(x,y):x+y=0P(x,y):x+y=0P(x,y):x+y=0 over R\mathbb{R}R.

  • ∀x ∃y P(x,y)\forall x\,\exists y\,P(x,y)∀x∃yP(x,y) is true — for each xxx, choose y=−xy=-xy=−x.
  • ∃y ∀x P(x,y)\exists y\,\forall x\,P(x,y)∃y∀xP(x,y) is false — no single yyy works for every real xxx.

When Quantifiers Commute​

Quantifiers of the same type can switch order:

∀x∀y P(x,y)≡∀y∀x P(x,y)and∃x∃y P(x,y)≡∃y∃x P(x,y)\forall x\forall y\,P(x,y)\equiv \forall y\forall x\,P(x,y) \qquad\text{and}\qquad \exists x\exists y\,P(x,y)\equiv \exists y\exists x\,P(x,y)∀x∀yP(x,y)≡∀y∀xP(x,y)and∃x∃yP(x,y)≡∃y∃xP(x,y)

However, ∀x∃y P(x,y)\forall x\exists y\,P(x,y)∀x∃yP(x,y) is generally not equivalent to ∃y∀x P(x,y)\exists y\forall x\,P(x,y)∃y∀xP(x,y). In short: ∀∀\forall\forall∀∀ and ∃∃\exists\exists∃∃ commute, but mixed quantifiers generally do not.

Negating Quantified Statements​

To negate a quantified statement, switch the quantifier and negate the predicate:

¬(∀x P(x))  ⟺  ∃x ¬P(x)¬(∃x P(x))  ⟺  ∀x ¬P(x)\begin{align*} \neg\big(\forall x\,P(x)\big)&\iff \exists x\,\neg P(x)\\ \neg\big(\exists x\,P(x)\big)&\iff \forall x\,\neg P(x) \end{align*}¬(∀xP(x))¬(∃xP(x))​⟺∃x¬P(x)⟺∀x¬P(x)​

For example, ¬∀x∃y∀z P(x,y,z)\neg\forall x\exists y\forall z\,P(x,y,z)¬∀x∃y∀zP(x,y,z) becomes ∃x∀y∃z ¬P(x,y,z)\exists x\forall y\exists z\,\neg P(x,y,z)∃x∀y∃z¬P(x,y,z) — every quantifier changes type.

Useful Denials​

A useful denial pushes the negation inward until it reaches the basic predicates. For example, negating ∀x∃y(P(x,y)  ⟹  Q(x,y))\forall x\exists y\big(P(x,y)\implies Q(x,y)\big)∀x∃y(P(x,y)⟹Q(x,y)):

¬(∀x∃y(P(x,y)  ⟹  Q(x,y)))≡∃x∀y ¬(P(x,y)  ⟹  Q(x,y))(switch quantifiers)≡∃x∀y(P(x,y)∧¬Q(x,y))\begin{align*} \neg\Big(\forall x\exists y\big(P(x,y)\implies Q(x,y)\big)\Big)&\equiv \exists x\forall y\,\neg\big(P(x,y)\implies Q(x,y)\big) &\qquad \text{(switch quantifiers)}\\ &\equiv \exists x\forall y\big(P(x,y)\land \neg Q(x,y)\big) \tag*{$\blacksquare$} \end{align*}¬(∀x∃y(P(x,y)⟹Q(x,y)))​≡∃x∀y¬(P(x,y)⟹Q(x,y))≡∃x∀y(P(x,y)∧¬Q(x,y))​(switch quantifiers)​■​

This is much more useful than leaving the implication negated.

How to Prove Quantified Statements​

Different quantifier structures require different strategies.

StatementStrategy
∀x P(x)\forall x\,P(x)∀xP(x)Start with an arbitrary xxx and prove the property; to disprove, find one counterexample
∃x P(x)\exists x\,P(x)∃xP(x)Give one witness
∀x ∃y P(x,y)\forall x\,\exists y\,P(x,y)∀x∃yP(x,y)Start with an arbitrary xxx and construct a suitable yyy (may depend on xxx)
∃x ∀y P(x,y)\exists x\,\forall y\,P(x,y)∃x∀yP(x,y)Find one fixed xxx and prove it works for every yyy

Two-Variable Predicates​

For a predicate P(x,y)P(x,y)P(x,y), the four canonical quantifier patterns are:

∀x∀y P(x,y)∀x∃y P(x,y)∃x∀y P(x,y)∃x∃y P(x,y)\forall x\forall y\,P(x,y) \qquad \forall x\exists y\,P(x,y) \qquad \exists x\forall y\,P(x,y) \qquad \exists x\exists y\,P(x,y)∀x∀yP(x,y)∀x∃yP(x,y)∃x∀yP(x,y)∃x∃yP(x,y)

Since ∀∀\forall\forall∀∀ and ∃∃\exists\exists∃∃ commute, swapping xxx and yyy in the first and last forms gives an equivalent statement. The two mixed forms are the ones that need separate analysis.

Unique Existence​

∃!x P(x)\exists! x\,P(x)∃!xP(x) means exactly one xxx satisfies PPP, which requires both existence and uniqueness:

∃!x P(x)≡∃x(P(x)∧∀y(P(y)  ⟹  y=x))\exists! x\,P(x) \equiv \exists x\Big(P(x)\land \forall y\big(P(y)\implies y=x\big)\Big)∃!xP(x)≡∃x(P(x)∧∀y(P(y)⟹y=x))

The first part says a solution exists; the second part says every solution must equal the chosen one.

Negating Unique Existence​

The negation of "exactly one solution" means either there are no solutions, or there are at least two different solutions:

¬∃!x P(x)≡(∀x ¬P(x))∨(∃x∃y(P(x)∧P(y)∧x≠y))\neg\exists! x\,P(x) \equiv \Big(\forall x\,\neg P(x)\Big)\lor \Big(\exists x\exists y\big(P(x)\land P(y)\land x\ne y\big)\Big)¬∃!xP(x)≡(∀x¬P(x))∨(∃x∃y(P(x)∧P(y)∧x=y))
Best Way to Remember

Not exactly one = zero or at least two.

Logic and Coordinate Regions​

Logical statements can describe regions in the plane. Suppose P(x,y):x+y>1P(x,y):x+y>1P(x,y):x+y>1 and Q(x,y):xy<0Q(x,y):xy<0Q(x,y):xy<0. Then P∧QP\land QP∧Q is the intersection of the two regions, P∨QP\lor QP∨Q is their union, and ¬P\neg P¬P is the complement of the PPP region:

Always check whether each boundary is included. For example, ¬(x+y>1)\neg(x+y>1)¬(x+y>1) becomes x+y≤1x+y\le 1x+y≤1, so the boundary line is included.

Negated Biconditional​

Since ¬(P  ⟺  Q)≡(P∧¬Q)∨(¬P∧Q)\neg(P\iff Q)\equiv (P\land\neg Q)\lor(\neg P\land Q)¬(P⟺Q)≡(P∧¬Q)∨(¬P∧Q), the required region is where exactly one of PPP and QQQ is true.

High-Risk Mistakes​

Necessary vs. Sufficient

If AAA is necessary for BBB: B  ⟹  AB\implies AB⟹A.

Negating an Implication

¬(P  ⟹  Q)≡P∧¬Q\neg(P\implies Q)\equiv P\land\neg Q¬(P⟹Q)≡P∧¬Q

Swapping Mixed Quantifiers

Generally, ∀x∃y\forall x\exists y∀x∃y cannot be changed to ∃y∀x\exists y\forall x∃y∀x.

Losing Quantifier Scope

Read the expression from the outside inward before manipulating it.

Forgetting Equality

Negating a strict inequality introduces equality.

Confusing Existence with Unique Existence

∃\exists∃ means at least one. ∃!\exists!∃! means exactly one.

BiconditionalP  ⟺  Q≡(P  ⟹  Q)∧(Q  ⟹  P)P\iff Q\equiv (P\implies Q)\land(Q\implies P)P⟺Q≡(P⟹Q)∧(Q⟹P)
Negated Universal¬∀x P(x)≡∃x ¬P(x)\neg\forall x\,P(x)\equiv \exists x\,\neg P(x)¬∀xP(x)≡∃x¬P(x)
Negated Existential¬∃x P(x)≡∀x ¬P(x)\neg\exists x\,P(x)\equiv \forall x\,\neg P(x)¬∃xP(x)≡∀x¬P(x)

Note that in general, ∀x∃y P(x,y)≢∃y∀x P(x,y)\forall x\exists y\,P(x,y)\not\equiv \exists y\forall x\,P(x,y)∀x∃yP(x,y)≡∃y∀xP(x,y).

If the structure of a statement is clear, most Midterm 1 problems become much easier.
