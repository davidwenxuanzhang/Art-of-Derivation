### Probability Mass Function


::::tip Probability Mass Function

We consider $\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{X}=x\mid C) $ as our central unit; then, with the shorthand: 

$$
M[x\mid \mathbb{X}]=\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{X}=x\mid C)
$$

$M$ is a **Probability Mass Function**.

:::info Properties

Combined with the axioms of probability, the probability mass function has several properties:

1. $0\le M[x\mid \mathbb{X}]\le 1$
2. $\sum_{(\text{all }x)}M[x\mid \mathbb{X}]=1$

:::
:::info Example 

Can $f(x)=kx$ be a probability mass function for some $k$ when $x\in \{-1,0,1\}$? 

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

No, because $f(-1)=-k$ and $f(1)=k$, which implies $k=0$

However, when $k=0$, $f(x)=0$, and $\sum_{x=-1}^{1}f(x)=0\neq 1$

</details>

<hr/>

Verify that $M[x\mid \mathbb{X}]= \frac{(1+x)^2}{15} $ is a probability mass function for $x\in\{-2,0,1,2\}$

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

- Verify $M[x\mid \mathbb{X}]\in [0,1]$

$$
M[-2\mid \mathbb{X}]=\frac{1}{15}\quad M[0\mid \mathbb{X}]=\frac{1}{15} \quad M[1\mid \mathbb{X}]=\frac{4}{15} \quad M[2\mid \mathbb{X}]=\frac{9}{15}
$$

- Verify $\sum_{(\text{all }x)}M[x\mid \mathbb{X}]=1$

$$
\frac{1}{15}+\frac{1}{15}+\frac{4}{15}+\frac{9}{15}=1
$$

</details>

<hr/>

Determine a value $k$ that makes $M[x\mid \mathbb{X}]=kx$ a probability mass function for $x\in \{1,2,\dots,n\}$

<details>
<summary><span className="math-keyword">Derivation</span></summary>

Clearly $k>0$

We need that:

$$
1=\sum_{x=1}^{n}M[x\mid \mathbb{X}]=\sum_{x=1}^{n}kx=k\sum_{x=1}^{n}x=\frac{kn(n+1)}{2}
$$
$$
\implies k=\frac{2}{n(n+1)}\qquad M[x\mid \mathbb{X}]=\frac{2x}{n(n+1)}
$$
Here, $k$ is called a normalization constant, and the process of determining $k$ is called normalization.

</details>


:::

::::

:::info Binomial PMF

The binomial distribution $\mathbb{X}\sim \operatorname{binomial}(N,p)$ has the probability mass function:

$$
\begin{align*}
M[x\mid \operatorname{binomial}(N,p)]&=\mathbb{P}[\mathbb{X}=x\mid \mathbb{X}\sim \operatorname{binomial}(N,p)]\\
&= \begin{cases}
\binom{N}{x}p^{x}(1-p)^{N-x},\qquad &x=0,1,\dots,N\\
0, &\text{else}
\end{cases}
\end{align*}
$$

Verify: $0\le M[x\mid \mathbb{X}]\le 1$ and $\sum_{x=0}^{N}M[x\mid \mathbb{X}]=1$

:::

## Expectation

::::tip Expectation

The **Mean** (or **Expectation**) of the discrete distribution $\mathbb{X}$ is:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{X}]=\mu =\sum_{\text{all }x}xM[x\mid \mathbb{X}]
$$

and the mean is a measure of central tendency.

:::note Notation

If there are multiple distributions/random variables, the random variable is often added as a subscript to $\mu$, such as $\mu_{\mathbb{X}}$

:::
:::info Example

Determine the expectation of rolling a fair, 6-sided die. All outcomes are equally likely: $\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{X}=x\mid \text{ELO})=\frac{1}{6}\text{ for }x\in\{ 1,2,3,4,5,6\}$

$$
\mu_{\mathbb{X}}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{X}]=\sum_{x=1}^{6}xM[x\mid \mathbb{X}]=\sum_{x=1}^{6}\frac{x}{6}=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{x=1}^{6}x=\frac{1}{6}\left[\frac{6(6+1)}{2} \right]=\frac{7}{2}=3.5
$$

The mean of $\mathbb{X}$ need not be an observable outcome for $\mathbb{X}$. Instead, think of $\mu_\mathbb{X}$ as a mean value over many observations.

<hr/>

:::

::::

:::tip Binomial Expectation

For a binomial distribution: $\mathbb{X}\sim \operatorname{binomial}(N,p)$

$$
\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{X}]=\sum_{x=0}^{N}xM[x\mid \mathbb{X}]=\sum_{x=0}^{N}x\binom{N}{x}p^{x}(1-p)^{N-x}=Np
$$
